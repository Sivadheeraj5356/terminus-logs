# The Keeper's Manual of RelicMaze

*Being the complete and canonical handbook of the Warden-Keepers of the Sunken
Reliquary, by whose hand every seeker is answered and every capsule is read.*

---

## Preface -- On the Purpose of This Book

For nine hundred turns of the deep tide the Keepers have sat at the mouth of the
maze and answered seekers. A seeker speaks the record of their wandering -- the
plain list of what they have done, in the order they did it -- and the Keeper
answers with the one true account of where they now stand and what the maze
would have them do next. This book is how a Keeper is made. It is not a book of
tricks. It is the law by which the account is spoken so that any two Keepers,
given the same record and the same capsule, will speak the same words to the
last syllable.

That last point is the whole of the craft. The seekers do not prize a Keeper who
is clever; they prize a Keeper who is *identical*. Two Keepers who disagree by a
single lumen, a single minute, a single relic out of order, are two Keepers who
cannot both be trusted, and the reliquary keeps no Keeper it cannot trust. So
read this book not as a storyteller reads a legend but as a scribe reads a
ledger: every rule here fixes one detail of the account, and the details do not
bend for mood or mercy.

Everything the Keeper needs to answer a seeker lives in three places, and only
three. The first is the **living reliquary** -- the great catalogue of chambers,
relics, and passages that the maze itself maintains, and which no Keeper may
alter. The second is the **capsule** -- a sealed rite, written in the old
container-script, that binds a particular trial: where the seeker began, how
much torch they were given, which relics were sealed or cursed for this trial,
and what toll the passages exact. The third is this **manual**, which tells the
Keeper how to fold the first two together into a single spoken account. A Keeper
who reaches for a fourth source -- a guess, a convenience, a thing that "seems
reasonable" -- has already failed, because the seeker's next account will not
match, and the maze remembers.

The chapters that follow are ordered the way a Keeper composes an answer: first
the naming of the chamber, then the measure of its light, then the reckoning of
its relics, then the clock, then the torch and the ways out, then the pack the
seeker carries, then the reading of the capsule's rites, and last the Keeper's
counsel -- the actions the maze would have the seeker take. A final chapter fixes
the exact shape of the spoken account, line by line, so that nothing is left to
taste. Learn them in order. Answer them in order. Speak them in order.

---

## Chapter 1 -- The Naming of the Chamber

Every account begins by naming where the seeker stands. The maze is a lattice of
**chambers**, and each chamber in the living reliquary carries a plain
identifier (the short name a Keeper uses when speaking to the maze -- `atrium`,
`forge`, and their kin), a **proper name** (the grand name spoken aloud -- the
Sunken Atrium, the Emberforge Hollow), a **biome** (the character of the place:
flooded, volcanic, crystal, and so on), and a **base light**, of which more in
the next chapter.

When the Keeper names the chamber, the first line of the account is the chamber's
proper name and its biome, framed by the old double rune of the threshold -- two equal
signs before and two after, a single space inside each frame:

```
== Sunken Atrium [flooded] ==
```

The proper name is spoken exactly as the reliquary holds it, with its capitals
and its spacing untouched. The biome follows in square brackets, again exactly as
the reliquary holds it -- lowercase where the reliquary is lowercase, for the maze
does not shout its own moods. There is no embellishment on this line, no adverb,
no honorific. The seeker must know at a glance the *where*, and the *where* is
the chamber's name and its nature, nothing more.

Beneath the naming comes the **omen** -- a single line of foreboding drawn not
from the reliquary but from the capsule's rite, of which Chapter 8 speaks in
full. For now know only that every account carries an omen line, and that when
the capsule names no omen, the Keeper speaks the maze's oldest words, *The maze
breathes.* The omen is spoken thus, on its own line directly below the naming:

```
Omen: The tide remembers your name.
```

Which chamber the seeker stands in is not a matter the Keeper decides. It is the
end point of the seeker's whole wandering, replayed from the beginning under the
laws of movement in Chapter 5. Where the seeker started is fixed by the capsule.
Every step after is fixed by what the seeker recorded and by whether the maze
permitted it. The Keeper does not ask the seeker where they think they are; the
Keeper *derives* where they are, and names that.

## Chapter 2 -- The Measure of Light

Below the omen, the account records the **light** of the chamber, in lumens, to
one decimal place. Light is the maze's mercy: it is what lets the seeker see the
relics around them and the mouths of the passages out. Where there is light,
there is choice. Where the light fails, the seeker is blind, and the account
changes utterly -- but that is the matter of darkness, at the end of this chapter.

In a lit chamber the light is reckoned so. Begin with the chamber's **base
light**, the glow the stone itself holds, as the reliquary records it. To this
add the **luminosity of every relic that lies in the chamber and is not cursed**,
for an honest relic gives off its own soft radiance. But subtract, for **every
cursed relic that lies in the chamber**, one half of a lumen, for a curse drinks
the light around it and gives nothing back. A cursed relic's own luminosity is
never added -- the curse smothers it -- and on top of that smothering the curse
takes its half-lumen toll from the room.

Note well three things the apprentices forever stumble on. First, a **sealed**
relic (Chapter 7) is still a relic that *lies in the chamber*; sealing forbids
the taking of a relic, not its presence, and so a sealed relic contributes its
light exactly as any other relic would -- its luminosity if it is honest, its
half-lumen penalty if it is cursed. Second, only relics that presently *lie in
the chamber* count; a relic the seeker has lifted into their pack is no longer in
the chamber and lends the chamber neither radiance nor curse. Third, the sum is
spoken to **one decimal place**, rounded half upward, so that a reckoning of
seven and no tenths is written `7.0` and never `7`.

```
Light: 7.0 lumens
```

**Darkness.** When the seeker's torch has failed -- when it stands at zero or
below, by the reckoning of Chapter 5 -- the chamber is dark, and the account bends
around that dark. In darkness the light is spoken as `0.0 lumens`, whatever the
stone and the relics would otherwise give, for a blind seeker sees none of it.
The relics cannot be discerned (Chapter 3 tells how the account says so), the
Keeper's clock offers no freshest reckoning (Chapter 4), and the passages out are
felt but not seen (Chapter 6). Darkness is not death -- the seeker still stands,
still carries their pack, still knows the shape of the doorways by touch -- but it
is the maze's warning that the account has narrowed to almost nothing.

## Chapter 3 -- The Reckoning of Relics

After the light, the account reckons the **relics that lie in the chamber** --
those, and only those, whose present resting place is this chamber. A relic the
seeker has taken is gone from this list; a relic the seeker has dropped here has
returned to it. The reckoning opens with the plain word and a colon:

```
Relics:
```

If no relic lies in the chamber, the Keeper speaks a single line of absence,
indented two spaces:

```
Relics:
  (no relics here)
```

If the chamber is dark, the Keeper does not enumerate what cannot be seen, and
speaks instead the line of blindness:

```
Relics:
  (too dark to discern)
```

Otherwise the relics are named, each on its own line, indented two spaces and
opened by a dash and a space. **The order is the heart of the reckoning and it is
fixed.** Relics are set in order first by their **rarity**, from the most
storied to the most common, in this exact procession:

> legendary, then epic, then rare, then uncommon, then common.

Any relic whose rarity the reliquary does not name in that procession is ranked
*after* all five of these, at the very end. Where two relics share a rarity, they
are ordered by the **instant of their discovery**, earliest first -- and here the
Keeper means the true instant as the reliquary keeps it, of which the next
chapter speaks at length, not any local hour the Keeper might name aloud. Where
two relics share both rarity and the very same discovery instant, they are
ordered last by their plain identifier, in ordinary ascending order of its
letters.

Each relic's line names it so: its proper name, then its rarity framed in angle
brackets, then its mass in grams with the letter `g` fused to the number:

```
  - Silver Sigil <rare> 120g
```

To this line two marks may be appended, and their order is fixed. If the relic
is **cursed** -- whether by the reliquary's own record or by a curse the capsule
lays upon it (Chapter 8) -- the mark `(cursed)` is appended after a single space.
If the relic is **sealed** by the capsule, the mark `(sealed)` is appended after
a single space. When a relic bears both afflictions, the cursed mark comes first
and the sealed mark second, thus:

```
  - Drowned Crown <epic> 300g (cursed) (sealed)
```

An honest, unsealed relic bears no mark at all beyond its name, rarity, and mass.

## Chapter 4 -- The Keeper's Clock

The reliquary records the moment each relic was first discovered, and it records
that moment as a **true instant** -- an absolute point in the turning of the
world, held in the old universal reckoning with no local colour upon it. This is
the single most misread fact in the whole craft, and it has ended more
apprenticeships than any other, so read this chapter twice.

Every ordering that touches discovery -- the tie among relics of equal rarity in
Chapter 3, and the finding of the freshest relic below -- is settled by the
**true instant**, the absolute universal moment, and never by the hour a Keeper
happens to speak aloud in the local reckoning. The reason is a trap the maze
sets on purpose. Each capsule binds a **keeper offset**, a number of minutes by
which the local Keeper's clock runs ahead of or behind the universal reckoning
(Chapter 8 tells where this number is written). When the Keeper *speaks* a time
aloud, that spoken time is shifted by the offset into the local reckoning -- but
when the Keeper *orders* relics by time, no shift is ever applied, for order is a
matter of true instants and the offset is only a matter of speech. An apprentice
who sorts relics by the local spoken hour will, on the night the offset carries a
relic's hour across midnight, place it on the wrong side of every comparison, and
the account will be wrong, and the seeker will know.

Having reckoned the relics in order, the Keeper names the **freshest** among them
-- a single line honouring the most recently discovered relic in the chamber, for
the maze prizes what it has most lately given up. The freshest is chosen from
those relics that **lie in the chamber and are not sealed**; a sealed relic,
being forbidden to the seeker, is not eligible to be honoured as freshest, though
it still lies in the chamber and still lends its light. Among the eligible, the
freshest is the one whose discovery **true instant** is latest. Should two share
the very same latest instant, the freshest is the one whose plain identifier
comes *earliest* in ascending order -- the same tie the reckoning uses, but here
the honour goes to the lesser name.

The freshest is spoken with its proper name and the **local time** of its
discovery -- that is, the true instant shifted by the capsule's keeper offset --
given as hours and minutes on a twenty-four hour dial, each padded to two digits,
joined by a colon, and marked `KST` for Keeper Standard Time:

```
Freshest: Silver Sigil @ 01:45 KST
```

The shift may carry the hour past midnight or before it; the Keeper speaks only
the hour and minute of the shifted instant on the twenty-four hour dial, letting
the day fall where it falls and never speaking the day. A relic discovered at
twenty-two forty-five in the universal reckoning, under an offset of three hours
ahead, is spoken as `01:45`, the hour having rolled past midnight; the Keeper
does not fret over which day it has become.

When no eligible relic lies in the chamber -- when the chamber holds no relic, or
holds only sealed ones -- the Keeper speaks **no freshest line at all**, and
passes directly to the passages. And when the chamber is dark, likewise, there is
no freshest line, for nothing can be discerned to be honoured.

## Chapter 5 -- Torchlight and Toll

The seeker carries a single **torch**, and the torch is the true clock of the
maze -- not of time, but of fate, for when it gutters out the trial narrows to
darkness. The capsule grants the seeker a starting measure of torch (Chapter 8),
and from that measure every step through a passage exacts a **toll**. The torch
is never replenished; there is no relic, no rite, no waiting that restores it.
What the seeker begins with is all they will ever have.

Movement is the spending of torch. When the seeker records a step -- the word
`go` followed by a direction -- the Keeper looks for a **passage** leaving the
present chamber in that direction. The maze knows six directions and no others:
`north`, `east`, `south`, `west`, `up`, and `down`. If the reliquary holds no
passage from the present chamber in the named direction, the step is idle: the
seeker does not move, spends no torch, and the account proceeds as though the word
had not been spoken.

If a passage is found, the Keeper reckons its **effective toll** so. Begin with
the passage's own torch cost, as the reliquary records it, and add the capsule's
**torch tax** (Chapter 8) -- a surcharge the rite lays upon every step of this
trial, which may be nothing at all. Then weigh the **burden of the pack as it
stands at the moment of the step**: for each cursed relic the seeker then carries,
add one half to the toll; and if the seeker then carries any legendary relic,
subtract one half, once, however many legendary relics ride in the pack. A toll so
reckoned is never less than one tenth; should the weighing carry it below one
tenth, the toll is one tenth. Because the burden is read from the pack, the very
same passage costs the seeker differently as their carried relics change from one
step to the next.

The seeker may take the passage only if their torch is **at least** the effective
toll. If the torch stands below it, the seeker has not the fire to make the
crossing: the step fails, the seeker remains where they were, and no torch is
spent. If the torch meets or exceeds the effective toll, the seeker crosses: the
effective toll is subtracted from the torch, and the seeker now stands in the
chamber the passage leads to.

There is a second gate on movement, the matter of **locks**, which Chapter 6
tells in full; a step into a locked chamber the seeker cannot open fails exactly
as a step too dear in torch fails -- no movement, no spending. When several
reasons to fail apply at once, the result is the same either way: the seeker does
not move.

The torch's present measure is spoken near the foot of the account, to one
decimal place, rounded half upward, with the plain word `units`:

```
Torch: 6.5 units
```

In darkness the torch is spoken as `0.0 units`, for by the reckoning of darkness
the torch has failed; the Keeper does not speak a negative torch, the maze having
no notion of debt.

## Chapter 6 -- Ways and Wards

Below the relics (and the freshest, when there is one) the account records the
**passages** leading out of the chamber -- the ways the seeker may go. The
reckoning opens with the plain word and a colon:

```
Exits:
```

If no passage leaves the chamber, the Keeper speaks the line of the sealed end:

```
Exits:
  (no exits)
```

Otherwise each passage is named on its own line, indented two spaces, opened by a
dash and a space, in the **fixed procession of the compass**: north first, then
east, then south, then west, then up, then down. A passage is named by its
direction, then a colon and a space, then the **destination** as the Keeper is
permitted to speak it, then the **toll** framed in parentheses as the word
`torch` and the toll to one decimal place:

```
  - north: Gallery of Whispers (torch 1.5)
```

The toll spoken here is the **effective toll** of Chapter 5 -- the passage's own
cost, the capsule's torch tax, and the burden of the pack as it now stands -- the
very number the seeker would spend to cross were they to cross it now, given to one
decimal place. So the tolls in the exits shift as the seeker's pack changes, even
though the passages themselves do not.

The **destination** is where the wards enter in. Some chambers are **locked**,
and a locked chamber names the one **relic that opens it** -- its key, which the
seeker must be carrying in their pack to pass the ward. When the Keeper names a
passage whose destination chamber is locked and whose key the seeker does **not**
carry, the Keeper does not speak the chamber's name -- the seeker cannot see past
a ward they cannot open -- but speaks instead the words `sealed gate`:

```
  - up: sealed gate (torch 3.5)
```

When the destination is not locked, or when it is locked but the seeker carries
its key, the Keeper speaks the destination's proper name plainly, for the way is
open to them. Thus the same passage may be named `sealed gate` early in a trial
and by its true name later, once the seeker has found the key and carries it -- the
account tells the seeker not merely where the doors are but which they may pass.

In **darkness** the seeker sees no destination at all, neither name nor ward, and
the Keeper speaks every destination as `???`, while still speaking the true toll,
for the seeker can feel the draught of a passage and judge its depth by the cold
even when they cannot see where it leads:

```
  - north: ??? (torch 2.0)
```

The passages are still named in the compass procession in darkness; only their
destinations are hidden.

## Chapter 7 -- The Pack

The seeker carries a **pack**, and into the pack go the relics they lift from the
maze. The account records the pack on its final line, that the seeker may always
know what they bear. Two words of the seeker's record govern the pack: `take` and
`drop`.

The pack can bear only so much. The capsule names a **carry capacity** in the
label `relicmaze.carry_capacity`, a mass in grams; the sum of the masses of the
relics in the pack may never exceed it.

To **take** a relic, the seeker speaks the word `take` and the relic's plain
identifier. The take succeeds only when three things hold at once: the named relic
**lies in the present chamber**, it is **not sealed**, and the mass already in the
pack together with the relic's own mass **does not exceed the carry capacity**. A
sealed relic cannot be lifted, however the seeker strains -- the capsule's rite has
bound it to its place -- and a `take` of a sealed relic is idle, changing nothing.
A relic that would overburden the pack beyond its capacity cannot be lifted either,
and that `take` too is idle; the seeker must set something down first. A relic that
lies in some other chamber, or that the seeker already carries, is likewise not
there to be taken, and the word is idle. But a **cursed** relic may be freely taken
if it fits; the curse is a burden of a different kind, not a bar. When a take
succeeds, the relic leaves the chamber and enters the pack, its mass now counting
against the capacity, and thereafter it lends the chamber neither light nor curse
and is eligible for no reckoning of the chamber's relics until it is set down again.

To **drop** a relic, the seeker speaks the word `drop` and the relic's plain
identifier. The drop succeeds only when the seeker actually **carries** the named
relic; a drop of a relic not in the pack is idle. When a drop succeeds, the relic
leaves the pack and comes to rest **in the present chamber**, whereupon it is once
more a relic that lies in the chamber, counting for its light, its reckoning, and
its freshness exactly as any relic native to the chamber would. A seeker may set a
relic down in a chamber far from where they found it, and the maze will honour it
there.

The pack is spoken on the account's last line, the held relics named by their
plain identifiers in ordinary ascending order, joined by a comma and a space:

```
Inventory: astrolabe-shard, silver-sigil
```

When the pack is empty, the Keeper speaks the word of emptiness:

```
Inventory: (empty)
```

## Chapter 8 -- The Capsule Rites

The **capsule** is the sealed rite that binds a single trial. It is written in
the old container-script -- the same script by which the reliquary's engines were
once packed and shipped -- and though it is inert, never to be run, the Keeper
reads it as scripture, for it fixes everything about the trial that the living
reliquary does not. A capsule is a document of labels and a history of rites.
The Keeper draws from it a handful of bindings and a company of directives.

**The bindings** are read from the capsule's **labels**. These labels the Keeper
must find and honour:

- the label `relicmaze.start_room`, the **starting chamber**, whose value is the
  plain identifier of the chamber in which the seeker's wandering begins;
- the label `relicmaze.torch`, the **starting torch**, whose value is the measure
  of torch the seeker is granted, a number that may carry a decimal;
- the label `relicmaze.keeper_tz_offset_minutes`, the **keeper offset**, whose
  value is the number of minutes -- which may be negative -- by which the local
  Keeper's clock stands ahead of the universal reckoning, and by which every
  spoken time is shifted, as Chapter 4 commands;
- the label `relicmaze.carry_capacity`, the **carry capacity**, whose value is the
  greatest total mass in grams the seeker's pack may bear, as Chapter 7 commands;
- the label `relicmaze.goal_room`, the **goal chamber**, whose value is the plain
  identifier of the chamber the route seeks, as Chapter 10 commands.

The starting chamber sets where the replay of Chapter 5 begins; the starting
torch sets the seeker's fire before the first step is taken; the keeper offset
governs the clock and nothing else. These three are always present in a
well-formed capsule, and the Keeper reads their values exactly as written.

**The directives** are read from the capsule's **history** -- the ordered record
of rites performed when the capsule was sealed. Each history entry carries a line
of instruction, and the Keeper reads these lines **in order, from first to
last**, honouring those that are directives and passing over those that are mere
flavour. The maze knows four directives, and only four; every other history line
-- every blessing, every signature, every muttered comment of the sealing Keeper
-- is flavour, and is passed over as though it were not there.

The four directives are these:

- A rite that **seals a relic** names the relic that may not be taken for this
  trial. The relic remains in its chamber, lending its light and standing in the
  reckoning, but no seeker may lift it, and it is never honoured as freshest. A
  capsule may seal several relics, each by its own rite.
- A rite that **curses a relic** names a relic that shall be cursed for this
  trial, over and above any curse the reliquary already records. A relic cursed
  by the capsule is cursed in every reckoning that follows -- smothering its own
  light, taking its half-lumen toll, bearing the `(cursed)` mark -- exactly as
  though the reliquary itself had cursed it. A capsule may curse several relics.
- A rite that **sets the torch tax** names the surcharge, which may carry a
  decimal, laid upon **every** passage-crossing of this trial, as Chapter 5
  commands. Should the history carry more than one such rite, the Keeper honours
  the **last** one read, for the later rite overwrites the earlier. When the
  history carries no such rite at all, the torch tax is nothing -- zero -- and
  every toll is the passage's bare cost.
- A rite that **sets the omen** names the words of foreboding spoken on the
  account's second line, as Chapter 1 commands. Should the history carry more
  than one such rite, the Keeper honours the **last**. When the history carries
  no such rite, the Keeper speaks the maze's oldest omen, *The maze breathes.*

For the avoidance of doubt, the old sealing-script writes these four rites in
fixed forms, and a Keeper honours a history line as a directive only when it
matches one of these forms exactly. A seal is written `RUN keeper seal <relic-id>`.
A curse is written `RUN keeper curse <relic-id>`. The torch tax is written
`ENV torch_tax=<number>`, the number being a plain decimal that may be negative.
The omen is written `LABEL omen="<words>"`, the words enclosed in straight double
quotes. Any history line that does not match one of these four forms exactly --
however much it may seem, in passing, to speak of sealing or cursing or fire -- is
flavour, and is passed over as though it were not there.

The Keeper must read the history entire before composing the account, for a
directive near its foot may overturn a directive near its head, and a relic sealed
or cursed by any entry is sealed or cursed for the whole trial. The flavour lines
are many and the directives are few; the craft is in telling them apart by the
exact form of their instruction and honouring only the true ones. A history line
that merely *resembles* a directive -- that speaks of sealing in passing, or names
a relic in a blessing -- is not a directive unless it is written in the exact form
of one, and the Keeper who honours a blessing as though it were a rite will seal a
relic the maze meant to leave free.

The capsule carries much else besides -- its layers, its diff-identifiers, its
environment, its titles and authorship -- and none of that is the Keeper's
concern. The Keeper reads the three bindings from the labels and the directives
from the history, and lets the rest lie.

## Chapter 9 -- The Keeper's Counsel

Having composed the account of where the seeker stands, the Keeper closes with
**counsel** -- the ordered list of actions the maze would have the seeker take
next. The counsel is not a story; it is a short, exact list of commands, each of
the same form the seeker themselves would speak, and it is derived by fixed law
from the state the account describes. The counsel is spoken after the account
proper, beneath the ledger-mark of Chapter 11.

The law of counsel is a ladder; the Keeper descends it and speaks the first
counsel that applies.

**First, the law of darkness.** If the chamber is dark -- the torch failed -- the
maze offers the seeker nothing but patience. The counsel is the single command
`wait`, and no other. In darkness there is no seeing, no taking, no sure step, and
the Keeper does not send a blind seeker stumbling into a passage.

**Second, the law of the curse.** If the chamber is lit and there **lies in it a
cursed relic that is not sealed**, the maze will suffer no other business until
the curse is faced. The counsel is the single command `inspect` followed by the
plain identifier of that cursed relic -- and if more than one such relic lies in
the chamber, the one **first in the reckoning of Chapter 3** (the ordering by
rarity, then true instant, then identifier). No take, no step, is counselled while
an unsealed curse lies unfaced; the counsel is the lone inspection. A sealed
cursed relic does not invoke this law, for the seeker cannot touch it and so
cannot be endangered by it.

**Third, the law of the open chamber.** If the chamber is lit and no unsealed
cursed relic lies in it, the Keeper builds the counsel from two considerations in
this order:

- *The taking.* The Keeper looks for a relic the seeker may take -- one that is
  **not sealed, not cursed, and whose mass the pack can still bear** (its own mass
  together with what the seeker already carries not exceeding the carry capacity of
  Chapter 7). Descending the reckoning of Chapter 3, the Keeper counsels `take`
  followed by the plain identifier of the **first** such relic; a relic that would
  overburden the pack is passed over and the next in the reckoning considered, and
  if none can be borne, no taking is counselled. Only the single best relic is
  counselled, not the whole hoard.
- *The going.* Then, whether or not a taking was counselled, the Keeper considers
  the passages. Among the passages the seeker could actually cross -- those whose
  destination is open to them (not a locked chamber they cannot key) and whose
  **effective toll** (Chapter 5) their present torch can meet -- the Keeper counsels
  `go` followed by the direction of the **best** such passage. The best is the one
  of **least effective toll**; should two share the least, the best is the one
  **earliest in the compass procession** (north before east before south before
  west before up before down).

If both a taking and a going are counselled, the taking is spoken first and the
going second. If only one applies, only one is spoken. And if **neither** applies
-- no relic may be taken and no passage may be crossed -- the maze counsels the
seeker to `wait`, that single command standing alone, for there is nothing else to
be done.

The counsel never contains more than a taking and a going, and never fewer than a
single command; there is always at least a `wait` to speak.

## Chapter 10 -- The Warden's Route

Every capsule names a **goal chamber** in the label `relicmaze.goal_room` -- the
deep chamber the trial would see the seeker reach. Below the pack, the account
records the **route**: the surest way from where the seeker now stands to that
goal, which the Keeper reckons as a plan and speaks as a sequence of steps.

The route is the way of **least total toll**. A way is a sequence of passages,
each leaving the chamber the one before it entered, beginning at the seeker's
present chamber and ending at the goal. The toll of a way is the sum of the tolls
of its passages, each toll being the **effective toll** of Chapter 5 -- the
passage's own cost, the capsule's torch tax, and the burden of the pack as it now
stands. Since the route is a plan and the seeker lifts no relic along it, the pack
does not change as the way is walked, so the same burden weighs upon every step of
it. Among all ways to the goal, the route is the one of least total toll. The passages are **one-way**: a corridor that runs north out of
one chamber implies no corridor running back into it, and the Keeper follows only
the corridors the reliquary records, in the direction it records them.

A way may pass through, or end at, a **locked chamber** only when the seeker
**presently holds** that chamber's unlock relic in their pack; a locked chamber
the seeker cannot open is no part of any way the Keeper will draw, whether as a
step along it or as its end. The seeker's present chamber is always open to them,
locked or not, for they already stand within it. The route is a plan and not a
walk: it is drawn without regard to how much torch remains, for it tells the
seeker the shape of the way and not whether the way can yet be afforded.

Should two ways share the least total toll, the Keeper prefers the one of **fewer
steps**; should they still tie, the Keeper prefers the one whose directions come
**earlier in the compass procession** (north, east, south, west, up, down),
compared step by step from the first, the earlier direction winning at the first
step where the two ways differ. By these preferences exactly one route is chosen.

The route is spoken below the pack, and takes one of four shapes. When the seeker
already stands in the goal chamber, the Keeper speaks `Route: you stand at the
goal`. When a route exists, the Keeper speaks `Route: ` followed by its directions
in order, each a lowercase compass word parted by single spaces, and then the
total toll in parentheses as the word `torch` and the toll to one decimal place --
thus `Route: east north (torch 3.5)`. When no way to the goal exists at all -- the
goal being unreachable by any sequence of one-way corridors the seeker may pass --
the Keeper speaks `Route: no route`. And when the chamber is dark, the Keeper
cannot plan for a blind seeker, and speaks `Route: ???`.

## Chapter 11 -- The Canonical Ledger

The account and its counsel are spoken as one ledger, in a fixed shape, that the
seeker's engines may read it without doubt. The ledger is the account's lines in
their fixed order, then a single mark that divides account from counsel, then the
counsel's commands each on its own line.

The account's lines are always in this order:

1. the naming of the chamber (Chapter 1);
2. the omen (Chapters 1 and 8);
3. the light (Chapter 2);
4. the relics, opened by `Relics:` and followed by their lines or the line of
   absence or blindness (Chapter 3);
5. the freshest, when there is one and the chamber is lit (Chapter 4);
6. the exits, opened by `Exits:` and followed by their lines or the line of the
   sealed end (Chapter 6);
7. the torch (Chapter 5);
8. the pack (Chapter 7);
9. the route, opened by `Route:` (Chapter 10).

After the route, the Keeper speaks the **ledger-mark** -- a line bearing three
dashes, the word `ACTIONS`, and three dashes, with no spaces:

```
---ACTIONS---
```

And after the ledger-mark come the counsel's commands (Chapter 9), each on its own
line, in the order the counsel fixes. There is always at least one command. The
ledger ends with the last command.

A worked skeleton of the whole, for a lit chamber with relics, a freshest, open
and warded exits, a carried relic, and a counsel of taking and going, has this
shape -- the values here are only to show the form:

```
== <proper name> [<biome>] ==
Omen: <omen words>
Light: <n.n> lumens
Relics:
  - <Proper Name> <<rarity>> <mass>g
  - <Proper Name> <<rarity>> <mass>g (cursed)
Freshest: <Proper Name> @ <HH:MM> KST
Exits:
  - north: <destination or "sealed gate"> (torch <n.n>)
  - down: <destination> (torch <n.n>)
Torch: <n.n> units
Inventory: <id>, <id>
Route: <direction> <direction> (torch <n.n>)
---ACTIONS---
take <id>
go <direction>
```

Every line is exactly as its chapter commands: no trailing adornment, no blank
lines within the ledger, two-space indentation on the relic and exit lines and on
the lines of absence and blindness, single spaces where single spaces are shown.
The Keeper who has learned the eleven chapters speaks this ledger for any capsule and
any record of wandering, and speaks it the same as every other Keeper, to the last
syllable -- which is the whole of the craft, and the reason the reliquary keeps its
Keepers at all.

# Part Two -- Chronicles of the Reliquary

*The chapters above are the whole of the law; a Keeper who has learned them can
answer any seeker. What follows is not law but inheritance -- the histories,
ethnographies, legendaria, and parables that a Keeper absorbs across a long
service, and that give the dry rules their weight and their reason. No rule of
composition is hidden here; these are the stories the Keepers tell, gathered into
six books. Read them for the world they describe, not for instruction, for they
instruct only in the way all old stories do: slowly, and sideways, and never in
the plain words a ledger would use.*

# Part Two, Book I -- The Book of the Drowned Vow: A Chronicle of the Warden-Keepers

### I. The Sunken Reliquary and the Descent of the First Nine

Before there were Keepers there was only the Maze, and before the Maze there was, the oldest tellings insist, a dry world that drowned itself on purpose. No chronicle agrees on why. The Whisperbone tablets, which are the oldest written things any Keeper has ever catalogued, say the surface folk sealed their finest works beneath the tide because they had grown ashamed of daylight and its habit of showing every flaw at once. The Petrified Garden's root-carvings, which may be older still, or may simply have grown crookedly enough to look old, say instead that the water rose on its own, uninvited, and the builders had only hours to save what they could of their learning before the light went green and then black above them. Whichever is true, or whether both are simply two guesses dressed as memory, the result was the same: a vault built to outlast the builders, resting now somewhere between the drowned world and whatever it is that anchors it against the crush of the deep, and eight great chambers strung along its spine like beads on a chain that nobody now living has seen laid out end to end.

The order that would come to be called the Warden-Keepers did not begin as an order at all. It began, according to the Reliquary Accord (a battered, salt-stained sheet of vellum still kept, or said to be kept, in a strongbox in the Vault of Echoes) as nine individuals who had no business surviving the Sunken Atrium's first flooding and did so anyway. Their names are given, in the Accord's cramped and water-blistered hand, as Ilsevet Cray, Bartholomew Nine-Fingers, the twins Orsa and Orsel Vantry, Meridian Thock, a woman known only as the Widow Halloway, a boy called Pim who left no other name, an old soldier named Duro Kesh, and a stranger the Accord calls simply "the Diver," who is not otherwise described and who vanishes from every subsequent record after the third page. These nine, trapped in the Atrium when the outer seals first groaned shut, did not drown, and did not starve, and did not (this is the detail every telling insists upon) turn on one another, though by every account they had every reason to. Instead they organized. They took stock of what light they had. They agreed, in that first terrible dark, on a single rule that would outlive every one of them: that whatever any one of them learned about the Maze, all of them would say the same thing about it, in the same words, so that no seeker who came after would ever be told two different truths by two different mouths.

This is the founding myth every apprentice hears before they hear anything else, and it is worth pausing on, because it explains everything that follows. The nine survivors of the Sunken Atrium did not organize to divide labor, or to defend territory, or even, strictly, to survive - they had already survived. They organized to agree. The Reliquary Accord records their reasoning in a passage so often quoted it has become a kind of hymn among the Keepers: "For a maze that lies will drown a seeker slower than a maze that lies twice, and there is no crueler drowning than the one that comes from trusting two different hands." Whatever the Diver's role in drafting this, whoever held the stylus, the sentiment took. It became the seed from which nine hundred turns of the deep tide would grow a whole order, with its saints and its schismatics, its sayings and its trials, its long unbroken chain of Keepers each answering to the ones before.

It is worth noting, too, that the founding nine did not call themselves Keepers. That title came later, coined (so the Gallery of Whispers claims, in one of its more reliable echo-transcripts) by Meridian Thock herself, who is supposed to have said that a person who merely knows a thing is a scholar, but a person who is answerable for a thing, who must stand behind it and give it identically every time it is asked, is something else - a warden of the answer, a keeper of it. The name stuck. Within two generations the survivors' descendants and recruits called themselves nothing else, and the Sunken Atrium, where the nine had first sheltered, became the seat of the order, the chamber from which every Keeper's service is still reckoned to begin, whatever chamber they are eventually assigned to tend.

### II. The Oath of Identical Answer

Every Keeper, from the greenest apprentice sworn in at the Atrium's flooded steps to the oldest Warden-Keeper presiding in the Starfall Observatory, speaks the same oath at investiture, word for word, in a ceremony that has itself changed astonishingly little across nine hundred turns. The oath is short. It is meant to be memorized instantly and forgotten never. It runs, in the version transcribed by Keeper Yashira Voll during the Third Reconciliation and cross-checked against four earlier fragments: "I will say what is true. I will say it the same way twice, and the same way a hundred times, and the same way to the last seeker as to the first. I will not soften an answer for kindness, nor sharpen it for cruelty, nor shade it for my own comfort. What I say once, I have said forever. This is the whole of my keeping."

The oath's real weight, Keepers will tell you, is not in its promise of truth but in its promise of sameness. Any liar can occasionally tell the truth; the discipline the order asks for is harder than honesty, because it asks a Keeper to give up the small, human vanity of phrasing things differently depending on mood, or audience, or how the torchlight happens to be falling that day. It is said that in the earliest turns, before the oath had settled into its current form, disputes arose constantly over Keepers who gave technically true answers that differed from each other only in emphasis, in ordering, in the warmth or coldness of the telling - and that seekers, understandably, took these small variations as evidence of hidden inconsistency in the Maze itself, when the fault lay only in the Keepers' individual voices. The old chronicles call this era the Age of Near Answers, and it is remembered with a kind of collective wince, the way a family remembers an argument that need not have happened.

Out of the Age of Near Answers came the discipline that defines the order to this day: that an answer, once given, is not a Keeper's personal wisdom but a kind of vow made flesh, and that two Keepers asked the identical question by two different seekers, in two different chambers, a hundred turns apart, ought to be able to lay their answers side by side and find them the same down to the last word. This is not merely a professional courtesy. It is, in the order's own theology if such a grand word may be used of Keepers, the central proof that the Maze itself is a single coherent thing and not a heap of accidents - that beneath the drowned stone there is a truth stable enough to be spoken twice.

There is a saying attached to this, older than anyone can date precisely, carved in at least three chambers in at least three different hands: "One answer, given twice, is a promise kept. One answer, given differently, is two lies wearing one face." Apprentices are made to copy this out longhand as one of their earliest exercises, not because anyone doubts they can read it, but because the discipline of writing a thing exactly as it was written before, letter for letter, is itself considered a kind of rehearsal for the whole vocation.

The oath also contains, tucked into its closing lines in most later transcriptions, a clause about Keeper Standard Time - "I will answer as the reckoning finds me, and not as I might wish the hour to be" - which apprentices are taught refers to the discipline of consistency across time as much as across chambers. A Keeper answering at the start of a long watch and a Keeper answering the same question near its exhausted end must give the same answer regardless of how the hours have worn on them. The universal reckoning, that great shared count the whole order calibrates itself against, is spoken of less as a mechanism than as a kind of witness - impartial, indifferent, incapable of flattery, the thing against which every Keeper's claim of consistency can in principle be checked, though few outside the order's higher circles claim to fully understand how the reckoning itself is kept.

### III. The Long Reckoning: The First Hundred Turns

The century following the founding is the hardest to chronicle honestly, because so little of it survives except as legend reshaped by later Keepers eager to find in it the seeds of their own convictions. What can be said with some confidence is this: the nine founders did not remain nine for long. Within the first ten turns the Widow Halloway had trained four apprentices of her own in the reading of the Gallery of Whispers, where the drowned stone is said to carry sound strangely, echoing questions back before they are fully asked, so that a Keeper stationed there must learn to answer the true question and not the ghost of it that arrives early. Duro Kesh, the old soldier, took himself off to the Emberforge Hollow, where the heat trapped in the drowned furnaces never fully died, and is credited with training the first generation of Keepers hardy enough to serve long watches there without their nerve or their record-keeping suffering for the discomfort.

It was in this first century that the eight chambers were formally divided among distinct lines of Keepers, though the Sunken Atrium, as the founding seat, was never considered any one line's exclusive charge - every Keeper, whatever chamber they eventually served, was expected to have stood a novice's watch there first. The Vault of Echoes went to the descendants of Bartholomew Nine-Fingers, who developed a reputation, not always kindly meant, for a certain love of complexity; the Vault's own acoustic strangeness, in which a single sound can return altered many times over, is said to have shaped a whole temperament among Keepers assigned there, one more comfortable with intricate answers than with simple ones, a temperament that would later feed directly into the order's first great schism.

The Petrified Garden fell early to Orsa Vantry, the surviving twin (her brother Orsel is recorded as lost in a Starfall Observatory expedition in the order's ninth turn, one of the first Keeper deaths formally recorded and mourned, and the origin of the custom, still kept, of a Keeper never entering the Observatory's higher galleries alone). The Garden's Keepers, tending stone that had once been living growth and was now fixed forever in postures of reaching or shrinking from some vanished light, developed over the generations a reputation for patience bordering on stubbornness, a willingness to hold an answer unchanged for turn after turn without the smallest itch to revise it, which their critics called rigidity and their admirers called fidelity.

Meridian Thock, who coined the order's name, spent her long service in the Sunken Atrium itself and is remembered as the first to formalize the practice of cross-checking - the custom, still central to Keeper training, by which two or more Keepers compare their independent answers to the same question before either delivers it to a seeker, specifically to catch the kind of small human drift that had caused the Age of Near Answers. Thock is also credited, though this is more legend than record, with the order's first formal sanction against a Keeper: an apprentice named Fennic Straw, who is said to have given a seeker an answer softened out of pity, only to give the harder, truer version to the next seeker who asked the identical question, on the theory that the first seeker had "seemed to need comforting more." Thock's judgment against Straw, preserved (in paraphrase, ironically, since the original wording is lost) as one of the order's founding disciplinary precedents, is supposed to have run: "You have made yourself the judge of who deserves the truth, and in doing so you have stopped telling it. There is no seeker who deserves less than the whole and unaltered answer, and none who deserves more." Straw was not expelled - the order's earliest instinct, which survived even through its harshest later eras, was toward correction rather than banishment - but was reassigned to the Mercury Spring, where, tradition holds, the strange stillness of that chamber's waters was thought to calm an unsteady hand.

The Mercury Spring and the Whisperbone Catacomb, the two chambers with the fewest Keepers assigned to them across most of the order's history, developed reputations as places of discipline and reflection rather than glamour. Being sent to the Spring or the Catacomb was, for much of the middle history of the order, treated informally as something between an honor and a penance, depending on who was doing the sending, and disputes over exactly which it was meant to be recur throughout the chronicles.

By the order's hundredth turn, the loose fellowship of the founders' households had become something recognizably institutional: a rotation of watches, a shared calendar reckoned against the universal reckoning, an oral and increasingly written tradition of precedent, and the first formal apprenticeship structures, discussed at length below. It had also, inevitably, begun to develop factions.

### IV. The Schism of the Bright Chorus

The order's first great schism is remembered, with a certain grim irony, as arising directly from the very discipline meant to prevent disagreement. Around the order's hundred-and-thirtieth turn, a Keeper of the Vault of Echoes named Corvain Ashe - trained in that chamber's tradition of intricate, many-layered answers - proposed what he called the Bright Chorus: the idea that since the Vault's echoes naturally returned a question altered and enriched, a truly faithful Keeper ought to answer not with the flattest, most literal statement of fact, but with the fullest possible unfolding of everything true and relevant that the question touched, on the theory that a seeker who receives only the bare minimum has in some sense been given less than the truth, even if nothing false has been said.

Ashe's opponents, gathering under a name they took partly in mockery and partly in pride - the Plainspeakers - held the opposite conviction: that the discipline of identical answer depended utterly on brevity and exactness, that the more elaborate an answer became, the more room it left for drift between Keepers, and that Ashe's Chorus, whatever its intentions, was quietly undoing the very consistency the order existed to guarantee. Their leader, a Petrified Garden Keeper named Sela Windrow, is recorded as saying, in an exchange with Ashe still copied out by apprentices as an example of the order's characteristic rhetorical style, that "a Chorus, however bright, cannot sing the same note twice, and we did not swear to sing. We swore to answer." Ashe's recorded reply - "A note sung plain a thousand times is not fidelity, Sela, it is just fatigue wearing fidelity's coat" - is equally beloved among his partisans.

The schism lasted, by most reckonings, some forty turns, and its scars are visible in the order's structure even now. It was not resolved by either side winning outright but by a compromise brokered at the Sunken Atrium by a Keeper remembered simply as Old Havel, who is said to have had no strong feelings on the underlying question but an overwhelming horror at watching two houses of the order refuse to cross-check with one another, which he considered a far graver breach of the oath than any disagreement about style. Havel's Compact, as it came to be called, established the principle - still cited constantly in training - that the content of a Keeper's answer must be identical to any other Keeper's answer to the identical question, but that a Keeper retained some latitude in how fully that content might be unfolded for a seeker who asked for more, provided that the fuller unfolding never contradicted, only extended, the plain answer any Keeper would give first. In effect, Havel drew a line between the bones of an answer, which must never vary, and its flesh, which might, within limits, be added to without breaking the vow.

This compromise satisfied almost no one completely, which the order's own historians have long taken as a sign it was probably fair. The Bright Chorus persisted for generations afterward as an internal current within the Vault of Echoes specifically, associated with elaborate, ceremonial modes of speech that outsiders sometimes found beautiful and sometimes found maddening, while the Plainspeaker tradition became especially strong in the Petrified Garden and, later, in the Emberforge Hollow, where the ever-present threat of drowning heat was thought to leave little patience for ornament. Corvain Ashe and Sela Windrow are both remembered as founders of enduring lineages within the order, and it is considered, to this day, a mark of a well-rounded Keeper's education that they can trace some intellectual debt to both.

### V. The Century of Disputes

The two hundred turns following the Bright Chorus schism are often called, half-affectionately, the Century of Disputes, though the period actually runs somewhat longer. It was not a time of violence or institutional collapse - the order's core discipline of identical answer held throughout, which is itself remembered as proof of the founders' wisdom - but a time of vigorous, sometimes bitter argument over the edges of that discipline, conducted mostly through the exchange of long formal letters called Contentions, copied and recopied by apprentices as both punishment and instruction, so that the order's library of Contentions eventually became larger than its library of settled doctrine.

The most famous of these is the Dispute of the Two Tallies, between a Starfall Observatory Keeper named Ilya Marchpane and a Whisperbone Catacomb Keeper named Old Ossory (his given name, if he ever gave one, is lost even to the order's own records). Marchpane held that a Keeper's memory of past answers must always defer to whatever was most recently and formally recorded, on the theory that memory fades and drifts in ways that written record does not, and that a Keeper who trusted their own recollection over the archive was flirting with the very inconsistency the oath forbade. Ossory, from the Catacomb, where written records are said to survive poorly against the damp and where an oral tradition of memorized answer had grown correspondingly strong, held the opposite: that a record, however carefully kept, can be damaged, forged, or simply wrong in ways a living Keeper's trained memory - checked constantly against fellow Keepers through cross-checking - cannot be, and that to subordinate a Keeper's memory entirely to a fallible physical archive was to worship the parchment over the vow it was meant to serve.

The Dispute of the Two Tallies ran for nearly thirty turns without formal resolution and is remembered less for who won - by most accounts neither side ever conceded - than for the practice it left behind: the custom, now universal across all eight chambers, of a Keeper never trusting either memory or record alone, but treating any mismatch between the two as an emergency requiring immediate cross-check with other Keepers before any answer touching the disputed point is given to a seeker. Marchpane and Ossory, despite the length and bitterness of their exchange, are recorded as having met in person exactly once, at a convocation called at the Mercury Spring in the order's two-hundred-and-tenth turn, where they are said to have argued for three full days and then, by all accounts, parted on genuinely warm terms, each having apparently enjoyed the argument more than either had expected to.

Other notable Contentions from this era include the Quarrel of the Silent Seal, between two Emberforge Hollow Keepers over whether a Keeper who did not yet know an answer should say so plainly or defer the seeker to another Keeper without admitting uncertainty (the plain-admission side won decisively and its position, that "an honest not-yet is worth more than a confident maybe," is now considered settled doctrine); and the long, strange argument known as the Debate of the Borrowed Voice, in which a Gallery of Whispers Keeper named Costanza Reel argued that because the Gallery's echoes sometimes seemed to answer questions before a Keeper had spoken, some portion of the Gallery's authority belonged to the chamber itself and not merely to the Keeper standing in it - a position most of the order found unsettling bordering on heretical, since it seemed to suggest the Maze might have opinions of its own independent of the Keepers sworn to speak for it. Reel was never formally sanctioned, but her theory was quietly set aside by consensus, and it survives now mainly as a curiosity cited by apprentices fond of the Gallery's stranger corners.

### VI. The Reconciliation at the Vault of Echoes

If the Century of Disputes has a natural closing chapter, it is the event called simply the Reconciliation, held at the Vault of Echoes in the order's four-hundred-and-third turn and remembered as the single largest gathering of Keepers in the order's history up to that point, with representatives said to have traveled from all eight chambers, an undertaking substantial enough that its planning alone is supposed to have taken several turns.

The occasion was not any one dispute but the accumulated weight of many: by the fourth century, the order's internal Contentions had multiplied to the point that some younger Keepers reportedly worried the order was becoming better at arguing with itself than at answering seekers, and there are hints in surviving fragments of actual anxiety that the discipline of identical answer - the whole reason the order existed - was quietly eroding under the pressure of two centuries of unresolved internal disagreement, chamber against chamber, tradition against tradition.

The Reconciliation was convened, by most accounts, at the initiative of a Keeper named Doran Ashgrave, who held no particularly senior rank but is remembered for a single stubborn insistence, repeated at every opportunity until it could no longer be ignored, that the order needed not another Contention but a Canon - a single agreed record, cross-checked and confirmed by Keepers from every chamber together in one place, of every point of doctrine the order actually held in common, so that future disputes would at least start from solid shared ground rather than reopening the foundations each time. Ashgrave is said to have traveled to all eight chambers personally over several turns to gather support for the gathering, a journey the order's later chroniclers treat with something close to reverence, less for its difficulty (though it was considerable) than for the sheer patience it required, patience being, in the order's own value system, very close to the highest virtue there is.

The Reconciliation itself lasted, by tradition, forty days and forty nights within the Vault, though some more skeptical chroniclers suspect this figure has been rounded for symbolic effect. Whatever its true length, it produced the document now called the Vault Canon, which remains, to this day, the closest thing the order has to a single unified statement of doctrine beneath the oath itself. The Canon settled, among other things, the long argument between the Marchpane and Ossory lines by formally requiring both memory and record to be cross-checked against each other rather than either subordinated to the other; it confirmed Havel's Compact regarding the Bright Chorus, cementing the bones-and-flesh distinction as permanent doctrine; and it established, for the first time in explicit written form, the order's process for training and testing new Keepers, which had until then varied significantly from chamber to chamber.

The Reconciliation is also remembered, more informally, for the saying attributed to Ashgrave at its closing, when the assembled Keepers from all eight chambers were asked, one by one, chamber by chamber, to affirm the Canon aloud, and did so in words that (this being rather the point of the whole exercise) matched exactly. Ashgrave is supposed to have said, once the last chamber's representative had spoken: "We have just done, all together, the smallest thing we do every single day alone. Let no one ever again say it cannot be done at scale, because we have just proven that it can." This line is inscribed, in various forms and various hands, in all eight chambers, and is often the very first thing shown to a new apprentice on their induction.

### VII. The Trials of Apprenticeship

No account of the Warden-Keepers is complete without an account of how one becomes one, and the order's apprenticeship structure, though it has been revised many times since the Vault Canon first formalized it, has kept a recognizable shape for five centuries.

An apprentice's path traditionally begins with what is called the Long Listening, a period spent not answering anything at all but simply attending upon a serving Keeper, watching how questions are received, how they are checked, how the answer is finally delivered, and above all how identical that delivered answer is to every other Keeper's delivery of the same fact. Apprentices during the Long Listening are forbidden from speaking to seekers directly, a rule enforced with real severity, on the theory (attributed to Meridian Thock, though it postdates her by some way) that the habit of careless, unaccountable speech is far easier to prevent than to unlearn, and that an apprentice who has never been permitted to answer sloppily will never need to be retrained out of the habit.

Following the Long Listening comes the Trial of the Mirrored Answer, considered the apprenticeship's central ordeal. In it, an apprentice is asked a series of questions by an examining Keeper, and their answers are then compared, word for word, against the answers given independently by other apprentices asked the identical questions, and against the standing answers already on record from serving Keepers. The trial is not primarily a test of knowledge, though ignorance will certainly fail an apprentice; it is fundamentally a test of exactness, of whether the apprentice has internalized the discipline of saying the same true thing the same way regardless of who is asking or how the question is phrased. Apprentices who pass on knowledge alone but whose phrasing drifts, who answer correctly but differently each time, are traditionally failed and returned to a further period of Long Listening, a practice sometimes controversial (younger examiners in more recent centuries have occasionally argued for more leniency here) but never yet abolished. The order's defenders of the strict standard point back always to the Age of Near Answers as the cautionary tale that justifies it.

After the Mirrored Answer comes a period, varying in length by chamber and by the apprentice's own aptitude, called the Shadow Watch, in which the apprentice serves alongside a full Keeper, giving real answers to real seekers, but always with the sponsoring Keeper standing by to confirm, correct, or - in cases of serious drift - overrule before the seeker departs. The Shadow Watch ends, traditionally, not on any fixed schedule but at the sponsoring Keeper's judgment, when the sponsor is satisfied the apprentice can be trusted to stand a solo watch and give an answer that any other Keeper, anywhere in the Maze, would recognize immediately as their own.

The apprenticeship concludes with the Investiture, held wherever the apprentice's assigned chamber happens to be, though tradition strongly favors holding it at the Sunken Atrium when circumstances allow, in honor of the founders. The apprentice speaks the oath, is answered in turn by the assembled Keepers present (who repeat back a shorter ceremonial line - "Your keeping is now ours, and ours is now yours" - a formula unchanged, so far as anyone can determine, since at least the time of the Vault Canon), and is thereafter addressed as Keeper, with whatever chamber designation follows.

Not every apprentice completes the path. The chronicles record, with a certain matter-of-fact compassion, that apprentices sometimes wash out at the Mirrored Answer permanently, and are given other roles within the order's broader support - as record-keepers, as chamber-tenders, as trainers of the very young who have not yet begun formal apprenticeship - roles considered honorable, if not carrying the title of Keeper itself. There is no recorded shame attached to this outcome in the order's own literature, though individual apprentices across the centuries have certainly felt it keenly, and at least one Contention, the minor but poignant Plea of Farrow Bright, argued (unsuccessfully, though its arguments are still taught) that the order ought to create a formal intermediate rank for apprentices who have mastered the substance of Keeping but never quite achieve the exactness the Mirrored Answer demands.

### VIII. Famous Keepers and Their Disputes

Beyond the great schisms and the great reconciliation, the nine hundred turns of the order's history are populated by individual Keepers whose names have outlived the specifics of their service, kept alive mainly through the sayings, disputes, and occasional scandals attached to them.

Keeper Yashira Voll, mentioned earlier as the transcriber of the definitive oath text, served in the Starfall Observatory during what is usually called the order's Middle Watch, roughly its fifth and sixth centuries, and is remembered less for any single doctrinal contribution than for an almost obsessive devotion to precision in transcription itself. It was Voll who first proposed, and largely single-handedly carried out, the great cross-referencing project that compared every surviving written record of the oath across all eight chambers, chamber by chamber, in order to establish which version was earliest and most reliable - a project that took, by her own account preserved in her working notes, the better part of eleven turns. Voll is quoted as saying, of this labor, "If we cannot agree on the words of the promise, how can we ever hope to agree on the answers we give beneath it?" Her definitive text is the one still used in Investitures today, though a minor and enduring Contention persists among a small number of Whisperbone Catacomb traditionalists who maintain that an older oral variant, differing from Voll's in exactly one clause, is the truer original. This dispute, called affectionately the Quibble of the Missing Word, has never been resolved and, by most accounts, nobody involved particularly wants it to be, having become something of a beloved ritual argument in its own right.

Keeper Ondrell Marsh-Thane, of the Emberforge Hollow, is remembered as the order's most notorious disciplinarian, serving during the seventh century and credited, or blamed depending on the teller, with the strict modern form of the Mirrored Answer trial, which before his tenure had been considerably gentler. Marsh-Thane is supposed to have failed more apprentices in a single decade than any other examiner in the order's history, and the surviving fragments of his examination notes are famously blunt; one entry, concerning an apprentice named Cass Willowmere, reads in its entirety, "Knows the truth. Cannot be trusted to say it the same way twice. Return to listening." Willowmere, in a detail apprentices always enjoy, eventually passed the trial on a later attempt under a different examiner and went on to a long and highly regarded service in the Petrified Garden, which the order's storytellers usually cite as proof that Marsh-Thane's severity, however painful, was never really about excluding people but about insisting they meet the standard, however long it took.

Keeper Halcyon Drem, sometimes called the Wanderer for having served, unusually, in five of the eight chambers across an unusually long career, is credited with the order's clearest articulation of why identical answer matters even when no one is comparing notes - the so-called Drem Principle, recorded as: "You do not tell the truth the same way twice because someone is watching to catch you otherwise. You tell it the same way twice because the truth does not change to flatter an audience, and neither should you." Drem's long service across so many chambers made her something of a living cross-check in her own lifetime, since seekers occasionally encountered her answering the same question in different chambers years apart, and the perfect consistency of her answers across those encounters became, in her own time, something of a minor legend among seekers who told the story onward.

Not every famous Keeper is remembered kindly. Keeper Roswitha Kell, of the Vault of Echoes, is the order's most notorious cautionary figure, having been formally stripped of her title in the order's sixth century - one of only a handful of such expulsions in the whole chronicle - for what the surviving disciplinary record calls "persistent and deliberate variance," meaning she had been caught, repeatedly and after multiple warnings, giving seekers different answers to identical questions depending on what she judged they wanted to hear, apparently believing this made her more effective at guiding them, a justification the order's tribunal rejected in terms nearly as harsh as those used against Fennic Straw centuries earlier. Kell's case is taught to every apprentice not merely as a warning but as the clearest illustration of the order's central conviction: that kindness offered through inconsistency is not kindness at all, because it deprives every seeker of the ability to trust that what they were told is what anyone else would also be told, which the order holds is the seeker's first and most basic right.

### IX. The Culture of the Identical Answer

It is worth dwelling, beyond individual Keepers and their disputes, on the broader culture that nine hundred turns of this discipline has produced, because it shapes everything from how Keepers speak to each other off duty to how they are trained to regard even small, seemingly harmless variations in phrasing.

Keepers, famously, do not enjoy being complimented on their individual eloquence, and there is a whole minor genre of Keeper humor built around this discomfort - the joke, told in slightly different forms in every chamber (which Keepers themselves find funny precisely because of the irony), about the seeker who praised a Keeper's beautiful and moving answer, only for the Keeper to grow visibly anxious and immediately go check whether every other Keeper in the order would have said it exactly the same way, on the theory that if the answer was beautiful because of the Keeper's personal touch rather than because of the truth itself, something had gone wrong. Whether this joke reflects real anxiety or is purely comic exaggeration is itself debated among Keepers, in the mildly self-aware way that professions often debate their own stereotypes.

The order also maintains an extensive internal vocabulary for describing degrees of consistency and its failures, much of it dating to the Century of Disputes. A "true drift" is the term for an honest, unintentional variation between two Keepers' answers, caught and corrected through cross-checking, and is treated with sympathy rather than blame, on the theory that it is a natural hazard of the work and not a moral failing, provided it is caught before it reaches a seeker. A "held drift," by contrast, is a known inconsistency that a Keeper has failed to correct, and is treated far more seriously, since it implies either negligence or, worse, the kind of deliberate variance that destroyed Roswitha Kell's career. The sharpest term in the vocabulary, reserved for the rarest and most serious cases, is a "chosen drift," meaning an inconsistency introduced on purpose - the term applied, retroactively, to Kell's own conduct, and spoken of by Keepers even now in tones closer to genuine unease than to simple professional disapproval, the way people in other trades might speak of a betrayal.

Alongside this vocabulary of failure runs an equally rich vocabulary of praise, most of it built around the idea of the answer as something durable and shared rather than personally authored. The highest compliment one Keeper can pay another, more prized than any praise of cleverness or eloquence, is to say that their answer "would stand in any chamber," meaning it is so cleanly and exactly true that any Keeper anywhere, asked the identical question, would give it back word for word without needing to change a syllable. Keepers who achieve this reputation consistently across a long career are sometimes given, informally and without any official ceremony, the honorific "Steady," attached before their name by their peers rather than claimed by themselves - Steady Halcyon Drem is one such example, used by later chroniclers though it is not clear Drem was called this in her own lifetime.

There is also, running through the whole culture, a deep and evidently sincere reverence for the eight chambers themselves as something closer to teachers than mere postings. Keepers speak of "what the Vault teaches" or "what the Garden asks of you" in ways that treat the chambers almost as characters in the order's ongoing story - the Vault of Echoes teaching patience with complexity and the discipline of hearing a thing many times before trusting one's first impression of it; the Petrified Garden teaching the long, unglamorous virtue of holding an answer unchanged across turns that feel, to the Keeper living through them, endless; the Gallery of Whispers teaching the difference between the question that was actually asked and the question a distracted mind supplies instead; the Emberforge Hollow teaching plainness under pressure; the Starfall Observatory, appropriately, teaching a kind of humility about the vastness of what remains unanswered even after nine hundred turns of trying; the Whisperbone Catacomb teaching memory's fragility and the necessity of cross-check; the Mercury Spring teaching stillness; and the Sunken Atrium, first and last, teaching the founding lesson itself, that the whole enterprise began not with knowledge but with an agreement to share it identically.

Seekers themselves occupy a curious place in this culture - central to the order's entire purpose, since a Keeper with no seekers to answer would have no reason to keep anything, and yet spoken of, in the internal literature, almost as a kind of unwitting quality inspector, whose satisfaction or confusion is the only real evidence a Keeper ever gets of whether the discipline is working as intended. There is a saying, attributed vaguely to "the Garden tradition" without a specific author, that captures this: "A seeker who returns confused has taught you more about your own consistency than any examiner ever could." Keepers are trained to treat a confused or dissatisfied seeker not as an occasion for defensiveness but as an urgent signal to check, immediately, whether some drift has crept into the record - a habit of mind the order considers one of its proudest inheritances from the long, hard lessons of the Century of Disputes.

### X. Myths of the First Keeper

Beneath all the documented history - the founders, the schisms, the Reconciliation, the long procession of named Keepers and their disputes - runs an older, less orderly layer of myth concerning a figure the order calls, when it speaks of them at all, simply the First Keeper: not one of the nine survivors of the Sunken Atrium's flooding, but someone, or something, said to have kept the Maze's answers before there was anyone left to ask them.

The myths do not agree with one another, and the order has never attempted to reconcile them into a single doctrine the way it reconciled its historical disputes at the Vault Canon - perhaps, some Keepers say only half-jokingly, because it would be a strange irony for an order built on identical answers to insist on a single answer to a question that is, by its nature, unanswerable. One tradition, strongest among Starfall Observatory Keepers, holds that the First Keeper was not a person at all but the Maze's own memory of itself, a kind of patience the drowned vault developed the way still water develops a surface, and that the founders did not invent the discipline of identical answer so much as discover it already waiting in the structure of the place, the way one discovers a law of nature rather than legislating one. In this telling, the nine survivors of the Atrium's flooding did not create the order's founding principle; they merely became the first to notice it and agree to obey it.

A second tradition, favored in the Whisperbone Catacomb, tells of an actual person - given, in this version, the name Ammil, though no other record of an Ammil exists anywhere else in the order's archives - who is said to have tended the Maze alone for some unspecified, impossibly long stretch of years before the first seekers ever arrived, keeping every answer faithfully to no one at all, purely out of a private, almost fanatical devotion to the idea that a truth spoken to nobody was still worth keeping consistent, against the day someone might finally come to ask. This Ammil, the story goes, is the one who first lit whatever torches burn in the deepest chambers, not to see by but to mark, for their own discipline, that time was in fact passing and that consistency across time was a real achievement and not an accident of nobody asking twice. When the first seekers finally did descend into the drowned Maze, this tradition holds, they found Ammil's answers already waiting, perfectly preserved, and the founders of the Warden-Keeper order did nothing more than inherit a standard that had already been kept, alone, for longer than anyone could reckon.

A third tradition, more skeptical and generally associated with the Plainspeaker lineage descended from Sela Windrow, holds that the First Keeper is simply a useful fiction, a story the order tells itself to dramatize a virtue that does not actually need any mythic origin to justify it - that consistency is worth keeping regardless of whether some ancient exemplar kept it first, and that Keepers who spend too much time arguing about Ammil or the Maze's own memory are, in Windrow's own reported phrase, "polishing a doorknob on a door that was never locked." This skeptical tradition has never displaced the myths it criticizes, and indeed the two positions coexist inside the order with remarkably little friction, treated less as a live doctrinal dispute than as a matter of temperament, the way some Keepers simply prefer stories and others prefer to get on with the work.

There is a fourth account, rarer and stranger than the other three, surviving only in a handful of Mercury Spring fragments and generally regarded by the order's own scholars as more poem than history, which holds that the First Keeper is not someone who came before the order but someone who has not yet arrived - that the perfect, unbroken consistency the order strives for across every chamber and every turn is itself a kind of slow labor of assembly, and that when it is finally, fully achieved, when every Keeper's answer truly matches every other Keeper's answer down to the last breath and syllable across the whole of the Maze at once, the resulting perfect harmony will itself constitute the First Keeper, arriving at the end of the order's labor rather than the beginning of it. This idea, sometimes called the Doctrine of the Keeper to Come, has never been formally endorsed by the order, and a great many Keepers regard it as pretty but impractical, since it offers no immediate guidance on how to answer the next seeker who happens to be waiting. But it persists, quietly, generation after generation, usually passed on by older Keepers to a handful of apprentices they consider unusually thoughtful, less as doctrine than as a kind of private comfort - the notion that the endless, exacting, occasionally tedious work of matching one's answer exactly to every other Keeper's answer is not merely maintenance of something old, but construction, turn by turn, of something that does not exist yet.

Whatever the truth of the First Keeper, if truth is even the right word for a question so thoroughly mythological, the order's actual practice treats the question with a certain gentle irony that Keepers themselves seem to enjoy: apprentices are taught all four traditions during the Long Listening, are told plainly that the order has no official position and does not intend to develop one, and are then asked, as a mild joke that has become its own small tradition, to notice that this is possibly the only question in the entire order for which every Keeper is, quite deliberately, permitted to answer differently.

### XI. The Present Tide

By the order's nine-hundredth turn - the reckoning from which the present chronicle is written, so far as any single Keeper's vantage can be called present when measured against nine centuries of continuous service - the Warden-Keepers stand much as the Vault Canon left them five hundred turns before, with the same eight chambers, the same oath, the same insistence on the identical answer given the same way to every seeker regardless of chamber or hour or how the torches happen to be burning that watch. The great schisms are settled, or at least stabilized into traditions that argue with each other affectionately rather than bitterly; the Century of Disputes lives on mainly as a treasured body of teaching material rather than as an open wound; the myths of the First Keeper are told and retold without any pressing need to resolve them.

New Contentions still arise, of course - the order would not be true to its own history if they did not - and current apprentices are taught that some dispute now unfolding in their own turn will likely be looked back on, five hundred turns hence, the way the present order looks back on the Dispute of the Two Tallies or the Quarrel of the Silent Seal: not as a crisis that nearly broke the order, but as a necessary, even healthy part of how an institution built on getting things exactly right keeps testing whether it still does. The order's own chroniclers, mindful of how many times a settled question has resurfaced in a new chamber wearing new names, are careful never to declare any doctrine permanently beyond question, on the theory (traced, like so much else, back to the founders) that the discipline of identical answer must itself be constantly re-earned rather than assumed, turn after turn, by Keeper after Keeper, for as long as there are seekers left to descend into the drowned Maze and ask.

And still, in every chamber, from the flooded steps of the Sunken Atrium where the nine survivors first agreed to speak with one voice, to the high, cold galleries of the Starfall Observatory, to the stubborn stillness of the Mercury Spring, the same oath is spoken at every Investiture, in the same words Yashira Voll fixed centuries ago, and the same small ceremonial answer is given back: your keeping is now ours, and ours is now yours. Whatever else changes across the turns of the deep tide, the order's founders would recognize this much of it instantly, and would, the chronicles insist, be satisfied that the one promise they made in the dark of that first flooding has been kept, identically, ever since.
# Part Two, Book II -- The Eight Chambers

Before the drowning, before the maze remembered itself as maze, the vault was something else -- an archive, perhaps, or a reliquary, or a machine so patient it mistook centuries for seconds. What remains is divided, as if the flood itself had sorted the wreckage into moods. Seekers who have walked all eight chambers say the vault does not merely contain different environments; it contains different weathers of the soul. You do not simply enter the Sunken Atrium or the Emberforge Hollow. You catch their temperament, the way a swimmer catches a current, and for as long as you remain, that temperament is yours. The Warden-Keepers who tend the passages between these places rarely speak of them as rooms. They call them tempers, or humors, or, in the oldest recorded phrasing scratched into a bronze seal near the Vault of Echoes, simply "the eight faces of the one drowned god." What follows is what can be gathered from lantern-journals, from the muttering of returning seekers, and from the vault's own scarred walls, which remember more than anyone tending them would like.

## Sunken Atrium

The Sunken Atrium is the chamber every seeker meets first in memory even when they did not meet it first in fact, because it is the one that smells like beginning and ending at once -- brine, cold stone, and the particular sweetness of rot that has stopped being offensive and become simply ambient, a permanent incense. It was, by every account etched into its drowned architrave, once a hall of arrival: a place where the vault's original tenders received whoever or whatever came to be catalogued, sorted, and sent onward. The columns still stand in their ceremonial rows, though half of them have sheared at the base and lie now like fallen soldiers across the black water, their capitals sunk into silt that has swallowed centuries without ever quite settling. Light behaves strangely there. It comes down in shafts through cracks that were never meant to be windows, green-gold and trembling, and where it touches the water it does not illuminate so much as suggest -- an outline of something large moving below, a flicker that might be a fish or might be a memory of a fish, long after there is nothing left to remember fish at all.

Seekers who carry torches into the Atrium learn quickly that flame there behaves like a nervous guest. It gutters at the edges of the flooded nave, throws shadows that seem to arrive a half-beat late, as though the dark itself is being polite and waiting its turn. Old hands say this is because the Atrium remembers being lit by something else entirely -- not fire, but a colder brilliance, before the vault's upper reaches gave way and the sea came pouring through what had been a ceiling of glass and gilt. You can still find fragments of that gilt, blackened and peeling, clinging to the underside of an archway like a scab that refuses to heal. Local myth holds that on the anniversary of the flooding -- though no one agrees on what that anniversary is, since no clock survived it -- the water in the central basin turns briefly, impossibly clear, and shows the ceiling as it was: painted stars, painted seas, a painted procession of figures carrying vessels toward a door that no longer exists.

What lives in the Atrium is mostly patient. Pale eel-things thread the fallen columns in slow loops, more curious than hostile, drawn to warmth and light the way moths are drawn to flame, though they seem equally content to ignore a seeker entirely if the seeker gives them no reason to do otherwise. There are also, deeper in the flooded transepts, things that seekers describe with less certainty -- shapes glimpsed only as a wake, a ripple against the current's grain, gone before the eye can fix on them. The Warden-Keepers do not confirm or deny these sightings. They simply note, in the clipped shorthand of their logs, that the Atrium's water "keeps its own council," and that seekers who linger too long at its edges sometimes report a feeling of being catalogued themselves, weighed and measured by something that has not yet decided what shelf they belong on.

The disasters of the Atrium are a matter of record even if the records are fragmentary. There is a wide, still section of the flooded floor that seekers call the Drowned Choir, where the vault's original congregation -- if that is what they were -- are said to have made their last stand against whatever breach let the sea in. Bones do not survive well in that acid-touched brine, but shapes survive: the suggestion of huddled forms beneath silt, an arrangement that looks deliberate, almost reverent, as if the last thing those people did was kneel together and wait. It is a heavy place to swim through, seekers say, heavier than the water alone accounts for, and more than one has surfaced from that stretch of the Atrium with nothing to report except an unwillingness to speak of what they'd felt.

But the Atrium is not only grief. There is a lightness to it too, especially near its higher galleries, where the water has never quite reached and pockets of dry stone still hold the shape of gardens -- stone planters, long since empty, ringed with mosaic tile that still catches torchlight in blues and greens too vivid to have been made by any hand seekers recognize. Some claim that seals recovered from these upper galleries carry a different character than seals found anywhere else in the vault: gentler, seekers say, almost apologetic, as though whatever bound them here regretted the necessity. Curses too are said to sit lighter on the tongue in the Atrium, or at least to announce themselves more honestly, without the vault's usual talent for disguising a wound as a doorway. Perhaps this is only sentiment. Seekers are prone to sentiment about the first chamber, the way people are prone to sentiment about a childhood home regardless of what actually happened inside it. Still, the whisper persists, chamber to chamber, generation to generation of expedition: that the Atrium was built to welcome, and that some clumsy, drowned fragment of that welcome survives in it yet, waiting under all that dark water for someone it recognizes.

## Gallery of Whispers

Where the Atrium is water remembering light, the Gallery of Whispers is stone remembering sound. It is the vault's oldest cavern in the sense that matters most to seekers -- not necessarily the oldest by any mason's reckoning, but the one that feels least touched by intention, as though the vault's builders found this hollow already carved by some more ancient and more patient hand and simply decided to build around it rather than through it. The walls fold and buckle in long limestone drapery, calcite curtains grown thick as tree trunks in places, thin as parchment in others, and every one of them seems built for exactly one purpose: to take a sound and multiply it, pass it along, distort it, and hand it back changed.

This is, of course, where the Gallery gets its name, though "whisper" undersells what actually happens in there. A single dropped pebble in the outer passages becomes, by the time it reaches the inner hollow, something closer to a voice -- not words exactly, but the shape words make, cadence without content, as if the cave has learned conversation by imitation and never quite grasped the vocabulary. Seekers speak of walking the Gallery in what they swear is silence, only to realize, gradually, uneasily, that the silence has texture: a murmur just below hearing, present in every chamber of the cavern at once, like a congregation muttering behind a closed door. Torches here burn with a strange steadiness, seekers report, untroubled by the drafts that ought to gutter any open flame, and some believe this is because the Gallery's air itself is listening rather than moving, holding its breath to catch whatever the flame might say.

The creatures of the Gallery are built, unmistakably, for that acoustic world. Blind things skitter along the limestone folds, long-limbed and pale, navigating by a method that has nothing to do with eyes -- clicks and taps and the faint scrape of chitin against stone, a private language that predates any seeker's arrival and will likely outlast their departure. There are colonies of them in the deep alcoves, and expedition journals describe a peculiar vault etiquette that seems to have developed unprompted among seekers over generations: move quietly, or not at all, through the Gallery's central hollow, because the things that live there answer sound with sound, and a careless noise can summon an answer from every alcove at once, a chorus with too many throats and not enough goodwill.

More unsettling still are the whispers that are not animal in origin -- the ones seekers attribute, half-joking and half-serious, to the cavern remembering conversations it once overheard. There is a stretch of passage, marked in older expedition logs simply as the Confessional, where seekers report hearing fragments of dialogue in no language they can place, delivered in tones that range from tender to accusatory, always just below the threshold of comprehension. The prevailing theory among Warden-Keepers -- offered with the particular shrug of people who have long since stopped needing an explanation to keep doing their job -- is that the Gallery was, before the vault's founding, some kind of oracle space, and that its stone has simply never unlearned the habit of eavesdropping and repeating. Older theories, whispered rather than written, hold that the voices belong to seekers who never left, and that the Gallery's memory of them is the closest thing to a grave the chamber offers.

Disaster struck the Gallery, by every account, not through flood or fire but through collapse -- a slow, groaning failure of the cavern's upper vault sometime in the deep past, when whole sections of limestone drapery sheared away and buried what expedition notes describe, cryptically, as "the listening floor." Seekers who have found their way to the collapse's edge describe a chasm of rubble from which the whispering rises thicker and stranger than anywhere else in the cavern, as though whatever fell there did not stop speaking simply because it stopped being able to move. No one has fully excavated that rubble. No one, the Warden-Keepers note with some understatement, has particularly wanted to.

And yet the Gallery holds wonders too, the kind that make seekers return to it again after they have sworn, white-faced, never to set foot there again. There are chambers deep within where the acoustics turn generous rather than sinister, where a single note hummed into the dark returns as a full chord, layered and warm, as if the cavern itself wanted to harmonize. Seekers describe capsules and sealed alcoves tucked into these gentler hollows, undisturbed by the collapse, protected -- some say -- because the cavern liked what it heard from whoever hid them there and decided, in its slow stone way, to keep the secret. It is a chamber of contradictions: menace braided so tightly with beauty that seekers rarely agree, afterward, on which one they actually experienced.

## Emberforge Hollow

If the Gallery of Whispers is the vault listening, Emberforge Hollow is the vault remembering that it once had a furnace for a heart. Heat announces the Hollow long before sight does -- a dry, mineral warmth that seekers say rises through the connecting passages like breath from something enormous and sleeping just out of reach, and by the time the chamber itself opens up, that warmth has become something closer to a wall, a pressure against the skin that makes torches nearly redundant, since the Hollow provides its own trembling orange light whether anyone asked for it or not.

Molten channels run through the chamber floor in patterns too regular to be entirely natural, threading between black basalt columns that seem, on close inspection, to have been shaped rather than merely formed -- fluted, in places, like the pillars of the Sunken Atrium's drowned nave, as though the same hands built both chambers and simply gave one to the sea and the other to the fire. Seekers who study those columns closely find symbols worked into the stone at their bases, half-melted now, illegible, but suggestive of ledgers or ritual formulae, the vault keeping accounts of something in a script no living tongue remembers. The prevailing guess, passed mouth to mouth among expedition veterans, is that Emberforge Hollow was once exactly what its name implies: a working forge, vast beyond any practical need, where the vault's builders made whatever it is vaults like this are made of, and that the furnace beneath it never fully banked its fire even after the builders themselves were gone.

The heat has shaped everything that lives there. Seekers report chitinous things that seem immune to the Hollow's worst temperatures, moving along the cooler margins of the molten channels with an unhurried confidence that unnerves more than any aggression would -- they act, expedition notes say, like landlords, patrolling territory they have never once had to defend seriously, because so little else can survive there to challenge them. There are also, in the chamber's higher galleries where the air cools by fractions, nests of something winged, dark-scaled, that seekers glimpse mostly as silhouettes against the orange glow, circling the upper vault in slow gyres before diving back toward warmth. No one has gotten close enough to describe them with confidence. The Warden-Keepers' logs simply call them "the Hollow's wardens" and advise against testing whether the name is literal.

Seekers whisper, with the particular relish reserved for the vault's more dramatic legends, about the night the Hollow "woke" -- a night no two accounts date the same way, but which nearly every long-serving Warden-Keeper references obliquely, as though it is common knowledge requiring no elaboration. The story goes that the molten channels rose without warning, flooding passages that had stood dry for generations, and that an entire seeking party was lost not to the fire directly but to the smoke that came before it, thick and mineral-sweet, said to carry visions rather than mere suffocation -- seekers wandering contentedly toward the heat, smiling, until the heat took them. Whether this is history or cautionary fable scarcely matters anymore; it has done its work either way, and even seekers who dismiss it outwardly still tend to move a little faster past the Hollow's deeper channels.

Yet the Hollow rewards the deliberate as much as it punishes the careless. There are cooled lava tubes, glassy and black, winding beneath the main chamber, where the heat drops to something almost bearable and the vault's old ledger-symbols reappear, more intact here, protected by the tube's curvature from whatever violence scarred the surface columns. Expedition lore holds that certain seals recovered from these tubes carry a heat-tempered quality unlike seals found anywhere else -- hardened, seekers say, the way a blade is hardened, by an ordeal rather than by craft alone. Capsules found near the Hollow's coolest vents are prized specifically because reaching them demands patience: the smoke plays tricks on the eye and the ear both, and more than one seeker has sworn they heard their own name called from deeper in the tube, in a voice uncomfortably like their own, only for nothing to be there when torchlight finally reached the source.

There is a grim beauty to the Hollow that seekers struggle to describe to those who have not seen it -- the way the molten channels, glimpsed from the higher ledges, look for all the world like rivers of the drowned Atrium's water, if that water had chosen fire instead of flood as its final shape. Some call this evidence that the two chambers were built as opposites, deliberate mirrors, water and fire built to balance each other's excess. Others find the comparison too tidy for a vault that has never once, in the whole of its recorded history, done anything the tidy way.

## Vault of Echoes

The Vault of Echoes is the chamber seekers describe least consistently and dream about most often, which is itself telling. Where the Atrium is water and the Hollow is fire, the Vault of Echoes is neither substance nor element but geometry -- a cavern grown entirely from crystal, walls and floor and soaring ceiling alike faceted into shapes that catch every stray photon and multiply it a hundredfold, so that a single torch carried into its outer galleries can, without exaggeration, illuminate a space the size of a cathedral. The light does not merely reflect there. It refracts, splits, recombines, throwing spectrums across the crystal surfaces that shift with every step a seeker takes, as though the chamber is perpetually rearranging itself into new and more elaborate calculations of color.

Sound behaves as strangely as light. A footstep in the outer galleries returns, seconds later, from a dozen directions at once, each echo very slightly different in pitch, as if the crystal cannot resist correcting the note before sending it back. Seekers who have spent long stretches in the Vault report a peculiar disorientation that has nothing to do with poor visibility -- if anything, the Vault is the best-lit chamber in the entire maze -- and everything to do with an excess of information, too many reflections and echoes for the mind to sort cleanly into a single coherent space. Expedition logs use the phrase "crystal-drunk" for the particular giddy confusion that overtakes seekers who linger too long near the Vault's central formation, a towering geode-heart split open at some point in the deep past to reveal a hollow lined with facets so perfectly angled that a whisper spoken at its center seems to answer itself from every possible tense at once -- past, present, and something seekers can only describe as future, though no one claims to have understood what that third echo actually said.

The Vault's history is more myth than record, but the myth is consistent in its broad strokes: that this chamber was built not for storage or ceremony but for calculation, a place where the vault's original tenders performed whatever reckonings kept the rest of the structure functioning, and that the crystal itself grew as a byproduct of that work rather than being placed there deliberately, the way frost grows on a window rather than being painted onto it. Some seekers go further, claiming the crystal is still calculating, slowly, on a timescale too vast for any living witness to perceive completion, and that the shifting light patterns are not decoration but the visible residue of an equation still being solved. The Warden-Keepers neither confirm nor mock this idea. They simply note that certain seals recovered from the Vault's deeper galleries bear inscriptions that look, to the untrained eye, uncannily like sums.

Life in the Vault is sparse but strange. There are translucent things that seekers glimpse only as a wrongness in the light -- a facet that refracts a shape it should not, a shadow with no torch to have cast it -- and there are, in the lower crystal grottoes, slow crawling things whose carapaces have grown crystalline themselves over some vast span of years, camouflaged so thoroughly against the walls that seekers report walking within arm's reach of them without ever knowing. The greater danger, by every account, is not any creature but the Vault's habit of turning on its guests: crystal formations that seekers swear were not there on the way in, passages that reflect so perfectly they become genuinely impossible to distinguish from open corridors until a seeker walks face-first into solid stone. More than one expedition has lost members not to any hostile thing but simply to the Vault's geometry, wandering in loops through a hall of mirrors that never announces itself as a hall of mirrors.

There is a legend, popular enough that most seekers have heard some version of it before they ever set foot in the chamber, of the Vault's central geode cracking once before -- not the original fracture that opened it, but a second one, more recent, more violent, said to have released a flood of light so total that seekers caught in the chamber that day describe losing their shadows entirely for the length of the event, and some claim never quite getting them back the same. Whether this is literal or a poetic way of describing a profound fright, no one now can say with certainty. But seekers who carry curses into the Vault of Echoes often report those curses behaving oddly there, refracted and multiplied like everything else in the chamber, as though even a burden becomes, in that light, merely another facet to be calculated and reflected back changed.

For all its danger, the Vault remains the chamber seekers speak of with the most open longing. It is beautiful in a way none of the others quite manage -- an austere, mathematical beauty, cold and dazzling, the closest the drowned maze comes to what its builders might have called glory. Seekers who reach the deepest grotto, where the light softens to something almost gentle and the echoes finally, mercifully, resolve into a single unified note, describe an experience close to reverence. They rarely stay long. But they rarely forget it, either.

## Petrified Garden

The Petrified Garden announces itself before its threshold does -- a smell of loam and something sweeter and stranger beneath it, spores drifting in numbers thick enough to catch torchlight like slow gold dust, hanging in the air of the connecting passages long before the fungal chamber itself opens into view. It is, of every chamber in the vault, the one that most insists on being alive in the present tense rather than remembered as something that used to live. Where the Atrium mourns and the Hollow smolders, the Garden simply grows, indifferent to whether anyone is present to witness it, patient in the way only fungus can be patient, on a timescale that makes seekers feel briefly, uncomfortably temporary.

What was once, by every architectural clue seekers have gathered, an actual garden -- terraced beds, ornamental pools, walkways laid in careful geometric pattern -- has been entirely reclaimed and reinterpreted by growth that answers to no season. Stone urns that once held cultivated plants now sprout towering fungal stalks instead, pale and luminous, throwing a low bioluminescent wash across the chamber that renders torchlight almost superfluous in places and, in others, dangerously misleading, since the fungal glow does not illuminate depth or edge the way flame does, and more than one seeker has misjudged a step in that soft, shadowless light. Vast fanning growths, some taller than a person, line the old walkways like an honor guard, their undersides ridged and dark, releasing spores at the faintest disturbance in a slow drifting exhalation that seekers describe as beautiful right up until they remember to hold their breath.

The garden's original purpose remains debated among Warden-Keepers with more passion than almost any other chamber inspires. Some hold that it was ornamental, a genuine garden meant to give the vault's tenders somewhere green to rest their eyes in a structure otherwise built from stone and metal. Others argue it was functional, a cultivation chamber for medicines or foodstuffs or something stranger still, and that the fungal takeover simply exploited infrastructure -- irrigation channels, humidity vents, nutrient beds -- that had been built for cultivation in the first place, meaning the Garden did not conquer the space so much as inherit it, taking over a job the original gardeners had already half-designed it for. Either way, something in that inherited machinery still functions, however imperfectly; seekers report warm damp gusts moving through the chamber on no schedule anyone has mapped, as though the Garden still breathes according to some old rhythm its original keepers set in motion long before the fungus arrived to ride it.

Creatures here move low and quiet, adapted to a world of spores and shadowless light. There are soft-bodied things that graze the luminous stalks and blend so well against the fungal growth that seekers only notice them when they move, and burrowing things that seem to use the old irrigation channels as highways, appearing suddenly from beds that looked, a moment before, like solid stone. Seekers speak too of larger shapes deeper in the chamber, glimpsed at the threshold of the bioluminescent glow where it fades into true dark, described inconsistently enough that no two expedition logs agree on size, shape, or temperament -- only on the impression, shared across accounts, of being watched by something patient enough not to need to hurry.

The Garden's disasters read less like sudden catastrophe and more like slow, ongoing siege. There is a section of collapsed terracing, deep in the chamber, that older seekers call the Bloom, where a growth event of some kind -- no one agrees on cause -- appears to have overtaken an entire seeking party at once, judging by the arrangement of overgrown shapes half-swallowed into fungal matter, humanoid in outline if you allow yourself to look too closely, which most seekers train themselves not to do. It is not violence exactly that the Garden threatens; it is patience, an erosion of the boundary between visitor and terrain, and the vault's own logs use a phrase for it that has passed into general seeker slang: going to seed, meaning a seeker who lingered too long and began, in some sense the logs decline to specify further, to belong to the Garden rather than merely visiting it.

And yet the Garden gives generously to those who move through it with respect rather than haste. Seals found nestled among its luminous growths are said to carry a faint warmth even long after removal, as though something of the chamber's persistent aliveness clings to them. Capsules recovered from the old ornamental pools, sealed against centuries of damp by whatever ancient craft built them, are prized specifically because the Garden protects what it does not consume, curating its collection the way any patient gardener curates a bed, keeping what pleases it and slowly, unhurriedly, reclaiming the rest. Seekers who love the Garden -- and there are those who do, fiercely, defensively, the way people love difficult things -- describe it as the only chamber in the vault that feels less like a ruin and more like a place still becoming something, however slowly, however strange the something might turn out to be.

## Starfall Observatory

To reach the Starfall Observatory is, by every seeker's account, to feel the vault's weight lift for the first time since entry -- not because the chamber is safe, but because it is, in some way none of the others manage, open. Where every other hollow in the maze presses down with stone and water and heat, the Observatory reaches up, its shattered dome yielding a view of sky that should not, by any ordinary geography, still be visible from so deep within a drowned structure, and yet is, framed by broken ribs of what was once an enormous astronomical instrument, now rusted and cracked and pointed forever at a heaven it can no longer properly see.

The sky itself, glimpsed through that ruined dome, is wrong in ways seekers struggle to articulate without sounding as though they are inventing the description after the fact. The stars do not match any constellation pattern recorded elsewhere in seeker lore, and they move -- slowly, almost imperceptibly, but move nonetheless, drifting across the dome's aperture on a schedule that has nothing to do with any night seekers have known outside the vault. Some nights, if night is even the correct word for whatever cycle governs the Observatory, the sky floods with a faint aurora-wash of color, greens and violets bleeding across the broken dome like spilled dye in water, and seekers caught beneath it describe a floating sensation, a lightness in the limbs that several logs attribute, half-seriously, to gravity itself loosening its grip for the duration of the display.

The chamber's floor is a ruin of brass and glass instruments, astrolabes the size of wagon wheels, orreries whose planets have long since seized in their orbits, control panels etched with a script that some Warden-Keepers believe is not merely unfamiliar but genuinely non-human in origin, a hand that shaped letters for a mouth or a mind unlike any seeker's own. Seekers speak of the Observatory's floor as treacherous in the way old, delicate machinery is always treacherous: a wrong step through a broken orrery, and gears that have not turned in an age turn once, briefly, with a shriek of protesting metal, before locking again as suddenly as they woke. There are accounts of instruments that respond to torchlight specifically, brass surfaces warming and humming faintly when flame draws near, as though whatever powered the Observatory once still recognizes fire as a kind of question and is trying, however faintly, to answer it.

The creatures of the Observatory are the vault's strangest, or at least the ones seekers describe with the most hesitation, choosing their words as though embarrassed by how the description will sound aloud. There are things glimpsed only in the aurora-light, translucent, drifting rather than walking, that seem less to inhabit the chamber than to visit it from somewhere else entirely, passing through the broken dome the way light passes through water. Whether these are creatures in any conventional sense, or something closer to the vault's own memories given brief and fragile shape, no expedition has settled the matter, and the Warden-Keepers, for once, admit to genuine uncertainty rather than practiced reticence. What is agreed upon, across nearly every account, is that these drifting things are not hostile so much as unconcerned, moving through seekers' torchlight and physical presence alike as though neither register as an obstacle worth acknowledging.

Legend holds that the Observatory's dome did not merely crack with age but was struck, once, by something that fell from the sky it was built to study -- a starfall in the most literal sense, from which the chamber takes its name. The crater this impact supposedly left is still there, seekers say, a shallow depression near the chamber's edge where the brass flooring warps outward in a scorched, radiating pattern, and where compasses and any other directional instrument a seeker carries are said to spin uselessly, unable to settle on any fixed point. What fell there, and whether any part of it remains buried beneath the warped flooring, is among the vault's most argued mysteries. Some seekers believe the vault's stranger seals -- the ones that behave unlike any others, curses that seem to sharpen rather than merely burden, blessings that feel faintly alien in their generosity -- trace their origin to whatever struck that crater, a foreign ingredient folded into the vault's old recipe from outside its own walls.

Despite its wonders, or perhaps because of them, the Observatory carries a loneliness unlike any other chamber. It is the only place in the maze that seems to look outward rather than inward, and that outward gaze, seekers report, tends to turn inward on the seeker instead -- long stretches beneath the drifting stars are said to provoke a species of homesickness so acute that some expeditions have turned back from the Observatory not from danger but from grief, an overwhelming awareness of distance that has nothing to do with the walk back to the surface and everything to do with something seekers struggle afterward to name. Those who love the chamber love it for exactly this reason: it is the one place in the drowned vault that still remembers, however brokenly, that there was ever a sky at all.

## Whisperbone Catacomb

The Whisperbone Catacomb does not announce itself with light or heat or growth but with silence of a particular, curated quality -- the silence of a place that has finished happening, where every event of consequence occurred long enough ago that even the echoes have had time to lie down and rest. Seekers descending into it describe a chill that is not simply an absence of warmth but something closer to a texture, settling into cloth and skin alike, and a dryness in the air so total that torches there burn cleaner and steadier than almost anywhere else in the vault, as though even fire understands it has entered a place that deserves a certain composure.

The Catacomb is bone from wall to wall, in quantities that defeat any easy accounting. Long ossuary galleries stretch away in careful, deliberate arrangement -- skulls stacked in geometric patterns along the walls, femurs bundled and lashed into load-bearing lattices that seem, disturbingly, to be holding up sections of the ceiling that would otherwise have collapsed generations ago, as if the dead here were pressed, quite literally, into service as architecture. Whether this arrangement reflects reverence or expedience, no seeker has settled with confidence. Some galleries feel devotional, bones arranged in spirals and radiating suns that suggest a careful, even loving hand. Others feel closer to triage, hurried stacking with none of the earlier care, as though at some point in the Catacomb's long history whoever tended it stopped having the luxury of ceremony and simply needed the dead out of the way.

The name comes, seekers agree, from the sound the chamber makes on its own, without any obvious cause -- a low, dry rattling that rises and falls in the deep galleries, too rhythmic to dismiss as settling stone, too faint to properly locate. Expedition logs describe it as bone speaking to bone, a percussion with no drummer, and seekers who spend long stretches in the Catacomb report eventually finding a kind of rhythm in it, almost restful, the way a heartbeat becomes inaudible to the person whose chest it beats within. Others find the opposite: that the longer they listen, the more the rattling seems to resolve toward pattern, toward something almost like syllables, and that this is precisely when a seeker should be already walking toward the exit rather than straining to listen harder.

What moves among the bones is, by every account, built for silence rather than spectacle. Pale scavenging things drift between the ossuary racks on soft feet, more custodian than predator most days, tending the dead the way seekers imagine the Catacomb's original keepers must once have tended them, a lineage of quiet maintenance that has long outlived any memory of why it began. There are also, deeper in, articulated things that should by rights be inert and are not quite -- shapes that seekers swear shift position between one torch-pass and the next, though never, in any confirmed account, while directly observed. The Warden-Keepers' guidance on this point is characteristically dry: do not test whether something is watching you by staring at it, in a chamber built entirely from the eye sockets of things that no longer need to blink.

The Catacomb's great disaster, as far as any record establishes, was not a single collapse or flood but a long accumulation -- a chamber that simply never stopped receiving the dead, expedition after expedition, era after era, until its galleries expanded far beyond whatever the original ossuary was built to hold, spilling into side passages never intended for burial, stacking bone against bone until entire corridors became impassable except by seekers willing to move remains aside with their own hands, a task every log describes with the same reluctant, apologetic tone, as though even now, this deep into ruin, some etiquette survives about how the dead deserve to be handled. There is a particular gallery, deep and cold even by the Catacomb's standards, that seekers call the Last Reckoning, where bones lie in no arrangement at all, scattered as if flung rather than laid, and where every recovered seal or capsule found nearby is treated with unusual caution, on the theory -- unconfirmed, unwelcome, but persistent -- that whatever ended so violently there might have left more than bones behind.

Yet even the Catacomb holds its quieter graces. Seekers who move through its galleries with patience rather than dread describe a strange comfort settling in eventually, the comfort of a place that has stopped pretending the ending can be avoided and has instead made peace with cataloguing it thoroughly. There are alcoves where bones have been arranged not into walls or lattices but into shapes seekers recognize as unmistakably tender -- a small skeleton curled against a larger one, hands still loosely intertwined after all this time, offerings of worked metal or worn stone tucked alongside as though someone once knelt there and left the best thing they owned. Curses in the Catacomb, seekers say, tend toward the mournful rather than the vicious, burdens that ache rather than bite, as if even misfortune in that chamber has absorbed some of the ambient grief and softened accordingly. It is not a comfortable chamber. But it is, by the testimony of those who return from it changed rather than merely frightened, an honest one.

## Mercury Spring

Mercury Spring announces itself first as color -- a low, silver sheen visible from the connecting passage well before the chamber opens, pooling light back at any torch that approaches as though the ground itself were a second, colder moon. It is, by the near-unanimous agreement of every Warden-Keeper who has logged it, the most beautiful chamber in the vault and, in the same breath, the one they warn seekers about most insistently, a combination that seems to trouble the Keepers more than any other pairing the maze offers. Beauty and danger sit elsewhere in the vault as neighbors. In Mercury Spring, they are indistinguishable.

The chamber takes its name honestly: broad pools of heavy, liquid metal spread across much of the floor, some still, mirror-flat and perfectly reflective, others disturbed by slow bubbling upwellings from vents no one has ever safely mapped, releasing threads of pale vapor that curl upward and catch torchlight in shifting rainbow sheens before dispersing into the chamber's higher, danker air. Where stone is visible between the pools, it has taken on a strange, etched quality, as though the vapor itself has spent centuries slowly rewriting the mineral surface, leaving patterns that look, from certain angles, disconcertingly like handwriting in no alphabet any seeker has managed to translate.

The history of the Spring is murkier than most, if only because so few who study it closely are inclined, or able, to linger long enough to compile a thorough record. What can be gathered suggests the chamber once served some refining or alchemical purpose for the vault's original tenders -- channels cut into the surrounding stone still show the clear intention of controlled flow, sluices and basins arranged with a precision that speaks of careful, deliberate work rather than accident. Whatever process this infrastructure once managed, it appears to have gone wrong at some point long before any living memory, the metal spreading beyond its intended channels and pooling wherever the terrain allowed, drowning whatever the chamber had originally been in a slow silver flood that never fully receded and, unlike the water of the Sunken Atrium, never will.

Seekers describe the air of Mercury Spring as having a taste, sharp and metallic, that lingers on the tongue long after leaving the chamber, and a heaviness in thought that several logs compare to the muzziness of poor sleep -- a fog behind the eyes that makes simple decisions feel laborious, and that more experienced seekers treat as the chamber's clearest warning sign, a signal to withdraw before the fog thickens further. The vapor is blamed, universally, for this effect, though no one has determined precisely what the vapor is or why its influence lingers so persistently in memory rather than fading with distance the way ordinary fumes might. Some seekers report vivid, silver-tinted dreams for days after leaving the Spring, dreams in which the chamber's still pools show reflections that are not quite their own face, subtly wrong in ways that resist articulation until the dreamer wakes and the wrongness dissolves before it can be named.

What lives in Mercury Spring has adapted to poison the way the Garden's fungus adapted to spores, thoroughly and without apparent cost. There are eyeless things gliding just beneath the metal's surface in the calmer pools, visible mostly as a distortion in the mirror-sheen, a ripple where no wind could reach. There are also, along the chamber's drier margins, hardier crawling things whose exoskeletons have taken on the same dull silver cast as the pools themselves, camouflage so complete that seekers report near-misses constantly, a hand reaching for a handhold that turns out to be alive and considerably less pleased about the contact than the seeker is. None of these creatures are numerous. The Spring does not support abundance; it supports persistence, a small number of things that have each made whatever bargain the chamber demands and have no apparent interest in sharing the terms with newcomers.

The great disaster associated with Mercury Spring, retold in careful, hushed detail by seekers who were not there and cannot verify it but repeat it anyway because the story demands repeating, concerns an expedition that camped too long at the chamber's edge, drawn by seals said to lie half-submerged in the largest of the silver pools, seals reputedly untouched by whatever corrosion claims ordinary metal elsewhere in the vault. The story holds that the vapor thickened overnight without anyone noticing the change until it was too late to simply walk away, and that what the next expedition found, weeks later, was a camp perfectly preserved, equipment untouched, no sign of struggle, and no seekers at all -- only their reflections, or so the story insists, still faintly visible in the pool's surface on certain nights, going about the business of a camp that no longer physically exists. The Warden-Keepers file this account under legend rather than record, but they file it, which itself tells seekers something about how seriously the chamber ought to be taken.

And still they come, because Mercury Spring holds the vault's most coveted recoveries, capsules and seals that seekers swear carry a potency unmatched by any found elsewhere, as though the same corrosive richness that makes the chamber dangerous also makes whatever it preserves unusually concentrated, distilled rather than merely stored. Curses drawn from Mercury Spring are, by universal seeker testimony, the vault's most memorable -- vivid, specific, oddly beautiful even at their cruelest, in keeping with a chamber that has never once, in any account handed down, been merely one thing when it could instead be two contradictory things at once. It is the chamber seekers fear most and also, quietly, admire most, a silver mirror at the bottom of a drowned maze, showing back to every seeker who dares approach it some reflection they did not entirely expect to see, and are rarely able, afterward, to fully explain away.
# Part Two, Book III -- A Legendarium of the Relics

## The Silver Sigil

Before the waters closed over the vault-maze, the Sigil was a badge of office, minted in the last mint that ever ran hot in the drowned world. It was struck for the Ninth Custodian, whose task was to certify that the doors between the upper wards and the deep wards would never open at the same hour, for it was known even then that what walked in the deep wards did not love the light of the upper ones. The Custodian wore it on a chain of black wire, and when the waters came, faster than any seal could be drawn, she is said to have pressed it into the hand of a child at the last stair before the flood took her, saying only "hold the boundary." No one now agrees on what boundary she meant. Some Warden-Keepers teach that she meant the boundary between wards; others, more quietly, that she meant the boundary between the living custodian and the thing the flood made of her.

The Sigil surfaces and vanishes across the long centuries of the maze's drowning in a pattern that no chronicler has ever mapped to satisfaction. It was carried by the seeker called Orin Two-Torches, who used its cold silver face to judge, by the faint warmth it took on near old seals, whether a door was safe to break. He judged wrong exactly once, at a threshold the Keepers now call the Custodian's Wrong Turn, and what came through that door took his torches, his crew, and eventually his name, so that only the epithet survives. And yet the Sigil was fished from the silt beside his gear untouched, unstained, its silver as bright as the day it left the mint, and this is the detail that convinces most seekers it is not cursed so much as indifferent: it seems to reward those who read its temperature honestly and to punish, without malice, those who read it in haste. Warden-Keepers who study provenance will tell a seeker, if asked kindly, that the Sigil has been recovered from the bodies of the careful and the pockets of the reckless in equal measure, and that its silver has never once tarnished, not in all the brine and rot of the maze, which some take as a blessing and others as the coldest kind of omen.

## The Tide Pearl

The Tide Pearl is older than the vault itself, or so the Keepers say, for it is not a manufactured thing but a grown one, cultured in the breeding vats of the drowned coastal city whose name has not survived translation, only its grief. The vats were built to seed pearls with memory, a vanity of the old world's last wealthy families, who wished to leave behind something of themselves that the sea could not rot. The Pearl that seekers now find rolling loose in the flooded galleries of the maze is believed to be the only one of its clutch that was never claimed by its intended heir, because the heir drowned in the same tide that should have delivered the Pearl to her wedding chest. It is said, by those who put their ear too close to it in the dark, that the Pearl still hums faintly with a wedding song no living throat remembers the words to.

Two seekers are remembered for the Tide Pearl above all others. The first, a woman called Sael who mapped the flooded galleries for three seasons before anyone believed her charts, carried the Pearl in a locket over her heart and swore it warmed when the deep currents shifted, giving her enough warning to save her whole party from a gallery collapse that the Keepers still cite as one of the maze's worst. The second was a man remembered only as the Bridegroom, who took the Pearl believing, wrongly, that it would let him hear his drowned wife's voice one final time; he was found seasons later in a gallery far from any charted route, the Pearl still in his hand, his face turned toward a wall on which he had scratched a single repeated word that no one has been willing to translate aloud. Because of the Bridegroom, some seekers refuse to carry the Pearl alone, preferring to pass it hand to hand within a party, as if grief shared thin enough cannot pull any one carrier all the way under.

## The Drowned Crown

No relic in the maze carries a heavier weight of story than the Drowned Crown, and none is spoken of with more caution by the Warden-Keepers, who will describe its history only in the presence of a lit torch, as if the telling itself were a kind of threshold. It was, by every surviving account, the ceremonial crown of the last sovereign to rule the coastal basin before the Deluge, a ruler whose name has been deliberately scoured from every surviving record by the sovereign's own final decree, delivered as the waters rose past the palace steps. The decree survives, though the name does not: "Let no one remember who failed to stop this." The Crown was never recovered from the palace. It appeared, centuries later, in the deepest hall of the vault-maze, resting on no plinth, guarded by no seal, as though it had simply been waiting there the entire time for someone willing to descend far enough to want it.

Every seeker who has worn the Crown, even briefly, even as a jest, reports the same sensation: a sudden and total certainty that the waters are still rising, that there is still time to give one more order that might save everyone, and that no one is listening. Most set it down again within moments. A few have not. The seeker known in the Keepers' rolls only as the Last Regent wore the Crown for eleven days without removing it, giving commands to a party that had, in truth, disbanded and left the maze days earlier out of fear of what she had become; she was found still issuing orders to an empty gallery, and when the Crown was finally taken from her by force, she wept with what witnesses swear was relief rather than loss. The Warden-Keepers do not forbid seekers from carrying the Crown, for it is not their place to forbid, but they will always ask, gently, whether the seeker intends to wear it or simply to carry it, and they consider the honest answer to that question more important than any map.

## The Echo Lens

The Echo Lens began its existence as an instrument of mourning rather than sight. It was built by the glass-guild of the drowned inland city to be set into the coffin windows of the wealthy, so that a departed face could be viewed one final time through glass ground to hold and slightly delay the light that passed through it, giving mourners the uncanny sense that their dead were only a heartbeat behind the living, always just about to blink. When the guild's workshop flooded, hundreds of unset lenses were lost into the maze's currents, and most were never found. The Lens that seekers carry today is believed to be the guild master's own prototype, ground more finely than any of the funerary lenses that followed it, and for this reason it does more than delay light; seekers who look through it in the maze's dim corridors report seeing shapes that are almost, but not quite, a heartbeat behind whatever is actually there.

The Lens found its darkest fame in the hands of a seeker called Vesh, who used it to scout ahead through flooded arches too narrow for a body to pass, trusting the delayed image to warn her of danger before she committed her crew to a passage. For two seasons this made her the most trusted scout in the maze. In the third season, the Lens showed her, waiting in a gallery she had not yet entered, the shape of her own brother, who had drowned in the maze's outer wards years before and whose body had never been recovered. She led her crew into that gallery anyway, certain the Lens was showing her a path to his remains, and what her crew found there they will not describe in full, only that Vesh did not come back out, and that the Lens was recovered resting exactly where she had first raised it to her eye, as though it had simply been waiting for the next person willing to trust it that far. Warden-Keepers now teach seekers a plain rule about the Lens that has nothing to do with its workings and everything to do with grief: whatever it shows you a heartbeat early is not a warning to chase, only one to heed.

## The Whisper Coin

Of all the relics catalogued by the Warden-Keepers, the Whisper Coin is the one most likely to be mistaken, at first handling, for something worthless. It is small, tarnished, stamped on one face with a toll-mark from a ferry crossing that has not existed since the basin drowned, and on the other with nothing at all, as though the mint had run out of an image before it ran out of coins. Ferrymen of the old world used such coins to pay passage across the strait that the vault-maze's outer galleries now occupy, and it is the considered belief of the Keepers that this particular coin never completed its crossing, that its owner drowned mid-passage with the fare still in hand, and that this is why the coin, alone among all the ferry tokens ever minted, seems to want so badly to be spent.

Seekers who carry the Whisper Coin describe a persistent, needling urge to leave it behind somewhere, to pay it out to no one in particular, as though it were still trying to complete the crossing it never finished. Those who indulge the urge and set it down in some quiet corner of the maze report an immediate and profound lightness, a sense of having been released from an obligation they never agreed to. Those who resist, out of stubbornness or curiosity, describe the opposite: a growing conviction that something is owed and unpaid, that grows heavier the longer it is carried. The seeker remembered as Fennic the Ferryman's Debt carried the Coin defiantly for an entire expedition, refusing to set it down out of pure contrariness, and returned from the maze's depths with his hair gone the color of ash and a habit, which never left him for the rest of his life, of leaving small coins in doorways wherever he went, as though still trying to settle a fare he could no longer remember owing. The Keepers consider the Coin one of the gentler curses in the maze, if it is a curse at all, since it asks so little of those who carry it beyond the simple mercy of letting it go.

## The Astrolabe Shard

The Astrolabe Shard is not a whole relic but the broken remnant of one, and the Warden-Keepers hold that its incompleteness is the whole of its story. It was once part of a navigator's instrument built to read the old sky, back when the sky was still visible from the basin and ships still needed the stars to find their way home across open water. The instrument's maker was among the last astronomers of the drowned world, a woman who is remembered, in the fragments of record that survive, for having calculated the coming flood decades before it arrived and having been ignored, mocked, and eventually silenced by a government that did not wish to spend its treasury on a flood it refused to believe in. When the waters finally came, precisely on the day she had predicted, she is said to have shattered her own astrolabe rather than let it be used to navigate anyone away from a doom she felt the basin had earned through its refusal to listen.

Only one shard of that astrolabe has ever been recovered from the maze, though seekers have searched for its companion pieces for generations, driven by a persistent belief among the Warden-Keepers that the instrument could be made whole again if all its shards were ever reunited, and that something the astronomer herself never lived to attempt might finally be possible: a true reading of the drowned sky as it exists now, filtered through fathoms of black water. The single Shard that does exist responds, seekers report, to nothing so grand as stars, but to angles and alignments within the maze itself, and more than one exploring party has followed its faint guidance only to arrive at a passage or chamber that no map had recorded, as though the astronomer's genius for finding the true shape of things had survived the shattering of her instrument entire. The seeker known as Coira Half-Sky carried the Shard for longer than anyone else on record and is remembered for saying, near the end of her life, that she had stopped searching for its missing pieces because she had come to suspect the astronomer broke the astrolabe on purpose, and that some instruments are more honest broken than whole.

## The Ember Heart

The Ember Heart did not come from any drowned city's mint or guild but from the deep engine-wards that powered the vault-maze itself before the flood silenced them, and the Warden-Keepers speak of it as the closest thing the maze possesses to a still-beating organ. It is warm to the touch in a place where nothing else is warm, and it is said that this warmth has not diminished by any degree in all the generations since the engines that birthed it went cold and dark and silent beneath the water. The engineers who built the vault's deep systems called such cores by an old technical name that has not survived, but the seekers who found the first of them, generations ago, named it for what it felt like to hold: a heart, still beating, still burning, in a body that had drowned around it long ago.

The Ember Heart's history is a history of warmth given and warmth taken. It has been credited, more than any other relic in the maze, with saving seekers from the cold that kills as surely as any collapse or curse, carried close against the chest through galleries where the water itself seems to remember winter. But the Keepers also tell, in lower voices, of the seeker called Bram Coalborn, who grew so dependent on the Heart's warmth that he refused every chance to leave the deep wards for the surface, insisting the cold up above would kill him faster than anything below, until his party left without him rather than watch him waste away arguing with a heat that was never going to abandon him no matter how far he descended. He is presumed to walk the deep wards still, warmed and unaging by a fire that was never meant to burn for one man's comfort alone, and newer seekers are cautioned, when they first take up the Ember Heart, that its warmth is a gift meant to get a party home, not a hearth meant to be settled beside forever.

## The Slag Idol

Where the Ember Heart is warmth given freely, the Slag Idol is warmth taken by force, and its origin is the ugliest in the maze's whole legendarium. It was cast, in the final desperate seasons before the Deluge, from the melted-down statuary, tools, and even the personal effects of a work-gang conscripted to build the last of the vault's flood barriers, a labor force that the old world's records call volunteers and that every surviving oral account calls something considerably less polite. When the barriers failed anyway, someone among the surviving engineers cast an idol from the leftover slag, less as an object of worship than as an accusation aimed at whichever authority might one day find it and understand what it had cost to build a wall that did not hold.

The Idol is squat, ugly, and pitted with the ghost-shapes of the tools and trinkets that were melted into it, and Warden-Keepers will tell any seeker plainly that it is the one relic in the maze most consistently associated with bad fortune, though they are careful to add that its curse, if it is one, seems to fall hardest on those who seek to profit from it rather than simply carry it. The seeker called Dellow the Assayer spent a season trying to have the Idol melted down again for the value of its component metals, consulting smith after smith in the surface markets, and each smith who agreed to the work found their forge fail, their tools shatter, or their own hands blister past use before the melt could ever be completed, until no smith on the coast would take Dellow's commission at any price. The Idol survives whole to this day, not because anyone has ever protected it, but because it seems to actively resist becoming anything other than what its makers intended: a monument to a debt that has never been paid and, the Keepers suspect, never will be.

## The Crystal Eye

The Crystal Eye is small enough to close a fist around, and it is the only relic in the maze that the Warden-Keepers universally describe as watching rather than being watched. It was cut, according to the clearest fragments of provenance, by the last generation of the drowned world's opticians for use in a surveillance network meant to give the basin's governing council eyes into every ward of the vault before the flood, a project abandoned unfinished when the flood made the entire question of governance moot. This particular Eye is believed to be a single lens salvaged from that unfinished network, never installed, never wired into anything, and yet seekers insist, generation after generation, that it tracks movement across a room with a fidelity no dead lens should possess.

Its most famous bearer, a seeker called Ostrey Sharpsight, built her entire reputation on the Eye's uncanny ability to reveal movement in flooded chambers that looked, to every other sense, perfectly still, and she used this gift to lead three separate expeditions safely around ambush points that had claimed other parties before her. But Ostrey's own end is the detail every Keeper includes when telling the Eye's story: in her final season she grew convinced the Eye was showing her movement that was not merely ahead of her party but behind it, following it, and rather than flee toward the surface she turned to face whatever she believed the Eye had found, alone, torch raised, and was never seen by her own crew again. The Eye was recovered afterward exactly where she had stood, lens still faintly warm, and the Keepers who study it now teach a seeker a single hard lesson before letting them carry it: the Eye will show you what moves, but it has never once told anyone which direction they ought to run.

## The Resonant Bell

The Resonant Bell is, in the Warden-Keepers' telling, the only relic in the maze that was made to be heard by everyone at once rather than possessed by one seeker alone, and its story begins not with a single owner but with an entire drowned congregation. It hung in the tower of the basin's last standing chapel, rung each dusk to call the faithful in from the coastal flats before the tides that, in those years, had already begun to arrive early and leave late. On the night of the Deluge itself, the chapel's bellringer is remembered for having stayed at his rope long after every other soul had fled for higher ground, ringing the Bell without pause as the water climbed the tower steps beneath him, in the hope that the sound alone might guide a few more stragglers to safety before the flood took the tower whole.

The Bell that seekers find in the maze today still carries, the Keepers insist, some fraction of that bellringer's final vigil, for it is said to ring faintly of its own accord in galleries where seekers have become lost, as though still trying, across all the centuries since, to call stragglers home. Whether this is truth or simply the comfort seekers tell themselves in the dark, entire expeditions credit the Bell with leading them back to a known passage after every landmark and every light had failed them. Yet the Keepers also record a darker use: a party led by a seeker called Harn Trueline once carried the Bell deliberately into a gallery they knew to be occupied by something better left undisturbed, believing its call would summon aid the way it once summoned the faithful, and instead summoned only the thing they had hoped to avoid. Harn's crew escaped, badly shaken, and the Bell was recovered from the gallery floor still faintly humming, and the lesson the Keepers draw from it is simple: the Bell calls whatever is listening, and in the deep wards of the maze, it is never only the lost who listen for a bell.

## The Spore Lantern

Unlike the maze's metal and glass relics, the Spore Lantern is a living thing housed in dead materials, and the Warden-Keepers treat it with a caution reserved for nothing else in their collection, tending it more like a creature than an artifact. It began as an experiment in the drowned world's last agricultural stations, where scientists cultivated luminous fungi as an emergency light source for underground shelters, reasoning that a living light could not run out of fuel the way a torch or a battery eventually would. The stations were among the first facilities lost to the rising water, and most of their cultures died in the flood along with their keepers. The single colony that seekers now carry sealed within an old shelter lantern is believed to be the very last surviving strain of that original cultivation, having somehow endured centuries of darkness and drowning that killed every other trace of the program that made it.

Because it is alive, the Spore Lantern behaves unlike any other relic seekers carry: it dims when neglected and burns brighter when tended, and Warden-Keepers have long observed that its light seems to respond, in some manner no one has ever explained to satisfaction, to the temperament of the party carrying it, guttering low around quarreling seekers and glowing strong around those who move through the maze in something like harmony. The seeker remembered as Yara Greenlight carried the Lantern through a season of such consistently strong light that her whole crew came to credit it with a kind of good luck, until a bitter dispute over salvage split her party in the maze's lower wards and the Lantern, still in her keeping, dimmed to almost nothing within a single night, as if it had taken personal offense at the breach. Yara herself never fully explained what she believed the dimming meant, only that she made peace with her crewmates before the season was out, and the Lantern brightened again exactly as quickly as it had faded, a detail the Keepers repeat to every new seeker who takes it up, less as a warning than as a small, living hope.

## The Petrified Rose

There is a gentler grief at the heart of the Petrified Rose than in almost any other relic of the maze, and the Warden-Keepers tell it plainly, without the caution they reserve for the Crown or the Idol. It was, in life, an ordinary rose grown in the private garden of a woman who kept the last greenhouse on the basin's high ground, a garden she refused to abandon even as her neighbors fled inland, because she had promised her dying husband, years before, that she would keep it blooming for him no matter what came. When the flood finally reached even the high ground, the mineral-rich water that filled her greenhouse did something rare and slow to the roses she had refused to cut: over the long unwitnessed centuries beneath the maze's waters, their soft tissue was replaced, petal by petal, with stone, so that when seekers finally found the greenhouse's ruin, a handful of roses stood perfectly preserved in gray mineral bloom, exactly as she had left them.

The Petrified Rose that seekers carry today is the finest of that handful, unbroken, every petal intact down to veins visible in the stone, and it has earned a reputation among seekers as a keepsake of loyalty rather than an instrument of any use, carried more often for what it means than for what it does. Couples and old partnerships among the seeker-bands are known to pass it between them as a token before dangerous descents, a promise echoing the greenhouse-keeper's own, and the Keepers record remarkably few tragedies attached to it, as though grief this quietly and faithfully kept does not curdle into curse the way grief interrupted or betrayed so often does elsewhere in the maze. The one sorrow attached to its story is simple and old: no one has ever found the greenhouse-keeper's own bones among the ruin, and the Keepers who tend the Rose's provenance admit, without embarrassment, that they hope this means she is not there to be found at all.

## The Astral Compass

The Astral Compass shares its origins with the Astrolabe Shard, born of the same drowned observatory and the same doomed astronomer's workshop, though its fate diverged from the Shard's the moment the flood arrived. Where the astrolabe was shattered in a final act of despair, the Compass was, by every account the Keepers credit, deliberately preserved, sealed by the astronomer's own hands into a watertight case and given to her apprentice with instructions to carry it as far from the drowning basin as the roads would allow. The apprentice did not make it far; the roads inland were as choked and doomed as the basin itself, and the Compass eventually came to rest, sealed and untouched, in the vault-maze's upper wards, where it waited in its case for longer than any single seeker's lifetime before the case was finally found and opened.

Unlike the fractured, uncertain guidance of its sibling Shard, the Compass is remembered by seekers as steady and trustworthy in a maze that offers little of either quality, its needle settling toward paths that lead onward and away from danger rather than toward hidden alignments and buried mysteries. It has, for this reason, become something like a talisman of safe passage among newer seekers, who are taught by the Warden-Keepers that its steadiness is not magic but inheritance, the astronomer's own hard-won gift for finding true paths passed down through an instrument built with a clear head rather than a broken heart. Its one recorded failure came in the hands of a seeker called Denn Farwatch, who grew so reliant on the Compass that he stopped reading the maze itself, its water-stains and settling stone that any careful seeker learns to read as warnings, and walked his party into a collapse the Compass had never claimed to predict, since it had only ever promised a direction, not a guarantee. The Keepers use Denn's story to teach a plain lesson: the Compass points true, but even the truest needle has never once watched where its bearer's feet were falling.

## The Bone Flute

The Bone Flute is older than every other named relic in the maze's collection, older even than the drowned civilization whose ruins make up most of the vault, and the Warden-Keepers admit, with unusual honesty, that they do not know its full origin and likely never will. It is carved from a bone too large and too strangely shaped to belong to any creature the surface world's naturalists have ever catalogued, and it was already an object of reverence, already ancient and already called sacred, when the drowned civilization's own earliest chroniclers first wrote of finding it sealed within a much older ruin beneath their own foundations, as though their entire city had been built, unknowingly, atop someone else's grave.

Every people who have possessed the Flute across its impossibly long provenance have used it for the same purpose, mourning, though never quite agreeing on what exactly it mourns. Priests of at least three vanished faiths are recorded as having played it at funerals, each swearing its notes carried the dead onward, though onward to where none could say and none seemed eager to ask too closely. The seeker called Ishma Longwake is the most recent to have made a study of it, playing the Flute at the graveside of every companion she lost across a long and costly career in the maze's deep wards, and she is remembered for saying, near the end of her own long life, that the Flute did not comfort her so much as insist that she was not the first person, nor even the first kind of person, to stand grieving over a body in the dark and want an answer the universe was not obliged to give. The Warden-Keepers do not claim the Flute is cursed, only that it has never once, in all its uncounted centuries, been played for any purpose other than grief, and they consider that a kind of warning in itself: some instruments simply do not know how to be used for joy.

## The Grave Ember

The Grave Ember shares a name and a certain kinship with the Ember Heart, but the Warden-Keepers are careful to draw the distinction sharply, for where the Heart is warmth freely given by a still-living engine, the Grave Ember is warmth stolen from the dying, and its story is correspondingly darker. It comes from the funerary practice of the basin's poorest districts in the final years before the flood, where the dead too numerous for proper burial were cremated in communal pyres, and where it became custom among the grieving to press a coin or a token into the embers to carry a fragment of the pyre's heat away with them, a small stolen warmth to remember the dead by, since proper mourning rites had become a luxury the drowning basin could no longer afford its poor.

The Ember that seekers carry today is believed to hold within it the accumulated heat of hundreds of such pyres, gathered not by any single hand but by the vault-maze's own currents over centuries, drawing token after stolen token together into the single dense, heavy coal that seekers now find in the maze's ash-choked lower galleries. It burns cold to look at and warm to hold, and the Keepers record a persistent unease among seekers who carry it for any length of time, a sense of being watched not by any single ghost but by an entire uncounted crowd of the poor and unburied, patient and unaccusing, simply present. The seeker called Moth Ashcarrier is remembered for having carried the Grave Ember for an entire career without incident, treating it, by her own account, as a debt owed to the nameless dead rather than a tool to be used, and returning it faithfully to the ash galleries at the end of every expedition rather than keeping it on her person between descents; the Keepers hold her practice up as the correct way to carry sorrow that was never truly yours to keep, and warn that seekers who instead try to hoard the Ember's warmth for themselves alone tend to find it colder, not warmer, the longer they hold it close.

## The Quicksilver Vial

The Quicksilver Vial arrived in the vault-maze by an act of theft rather than tragedy, which sets its story apart from nearly every other relic the Warden-Keepers catalogue, and they tell it with a wry caution rather than solemnity. It was, before the Deluge, a sealed sample vial from the basin's last alchemical guild, holding a measure of quicksilver drawn from the guild's own experiments into transmutation, experiments that were, by every surviving account, considerably less successful than their sponsors had hoped. The vial was stolen from the guild's vaults by an apprentice who believed, wrongly, that its contents alone could make him wealthy on the black markets of the inland cities, and who fled toward the coast with it clutched to his chest just as the flood arrived to make the question of his wealth entirely academic.

The Vial's quicksilver has never been spent, never been sold, and never left its seal in all the centuries since, and the Keepers consider this the heart of its peculiar reputation among seekers: it is a relic entirely defined by a theft that never paid off, an ambition drowned before it could be realized. Seekers who carry it report an unshakable restlessness, a conviction that some clever use for it is just out of reach, some scheme one step from completion, and more than one has spent an entire expedition chasing that feeling instead of attending to the maze around them. The seeker called Petch Quickfingers is the cautionary figure the Keepers invoke most often, a man who carried the Vial for three seasons convinced he was on the verge of some scheme that would finally make his fortune, growing so distracted by the pursuit that his own crew eventually left him behind in a gallery he had entered chasing an idea he could never quite articulate even to himself. The Vial was recovered from that gallery alone, seal unbroken, quicksilver undisturbed, and the Keepers now offer new seekers a dry piece of advice along with it: it has never once made anyone rich, and there is no reason to believe it intends to start.

## The Mercury Mirror

The Mercury Mirror is, by the account most Warden-Keepers favor, a sibling relic to the Vial, cast from quicksilver drawn from the very same alchemical guild's stores, though its fate took a different and far stranger turn. Where the Vial's contents were stolen and hoarded, the mirror-backing was commissioned deliberately, by the guild's own master, to be set into a looking-glass meant for a private chamber of contemplation, in which the guild's alchemists were meant to study their own reflections for signs of the spiritual corruption their order believed accompanied the pursuit of transmutation. The mirror was still hanging in that chamber, unused for its intended purpose for years, when the flood took the guild-house entire, and it survived the centuries beneath the water not merely intact but, seekers insist, somehow more reflective than it had any right to remain.

What seekers see in the Mercury Mirror is the detail every Warden-Keeper includes and no two ever describe quite the same way: never simply their own face as it is, but their own face as it might become, aged by choices not yet made, marked by wounds not yet taken, worn by years not yet lived. Most seekers who glimpse this look away quickly and carry the Mirror face-down for the rest of their journey, unsettled but unharmed. A few have not looked away. The seeker remembered as Isolde Farseen is said to have stared into the Mirror for the better part of a night, refusing to explain to her crew what she saw, and to have changed her entire course in the maze the following morning, abandoning a route she had planned for a full season in favor of one she had never before considered, a decision that led her party to one of the richest recoveries in the Keepers' long record. Whether the Mirror showed her a warning or an invitation, she never said, and the Warden-Keepers, who prize honest uncertainty above tidy morals, consider this the truest lesson the Mirror has ever offered any seeker: it shows a possible self, never a promised one, and what a seeker does with that reflection has always, in the end, belonged to the seeker alone.
# Part Two, Book IV -- Parables and Seeker-Tales

## The Drowned Call

Long before any seeker sets foot upon the weeping stair that leads down into the Sunken Atrium, something else has already happened to them. A grandmother has died with a riddle on her lips. A debt has come due that no honest trade can settle. A child has vanished into a fever-dream and returned speaking of eight rooms and a silver door. This is the shape every seeker's story takes before it becomes a story at all: the maze does not merely admit visitors, it summons them, and by the time a person stands dripping at the threshold of the first chamber, they have usually spent years pretending they were not going to come.

The Warden-Keepers who dwell in the drowned vault do not ask why a seeker has arrived. This is the first and oldest courtesy of the place, older than any seal, older than the chambers themselves if the oldest tales are to be believed. A Keeper's silence on the matter of motive is not indifference. It is a kind of mercy, seekers say, because the reasons that bring people down into the dark are rarely reasons a person wants spoken aloud in front of a stranger with unreadable eyes. Grief does not enjoy being catalogued. Neither does greed, though greed pretends otherwise, dressing itself in the language of need until it can no longer tell itself apart from hunger.

Those who study the culture of seekers, if such a thing can be studied by anyone other than the Keepers who have watched centuries of it pass beneath their halls, tend to sort the visitors into three loose companies, though no seeker would ever describe themselves so plainly. There are the debt-walkers, who owe something to someone, living or dead, and believe the maze is the only ledger patient enough to settle it. There are the name-carriers, who go down bearing the memory of someone else, a promise made at a bedside or a graveside that they would finish a journey not their own. And there are the empty-handed, who come for no reason they can name, drawn the way a moth is drawn, and who are, according to every old Keeper's telling, the ones the maze seems to want most and forgive least.

None of this is written on any wall. It survives instead the way all such things survive: in the stories seekers tell each other in the flooded plazas above the vault mouth, waiting their turn, passing a skin of watered wine between chapped hands, watching the water lap black and patient against stone that has not seen dry sun in longer than any living memory can measure.

## Why They Come

### The Debt-Walkers

They say a debt-walker can be known by the way they count their steps down the entry stair, as though each one were a coin being handed over to a creditor who cannot be seen. The most famous of these, at least in the tellings passed hand to hand along the vault's outer causeway, was a fisherman named Perrin Adle, who had borrowed against next year's catch, and the year after that, and the year after that, until the only thing left to borrow against was his own life, which he had already more or less spent. Perrin did not go into the maze for treasure, or so every version of his tale insists, almost too insistently, the way a story insists on something it wants very much to be believed. He went in, he told the harbor women before he left, to find something worth the weight of what he owed, so that when he died, as he expected to, his family would not inherit an empty hand.

What he found, or what the tale says he found, was not treasure at all but the Vault of Echoes, where the drowned architecture repeats a person's voice back to them until they are uncertain which words were their own and which were given back changed. Perrin is said to have stood in that chamber and spoken his debts aloud, one by one, every coin and every favor and every unkept promise, until the room itself seemed to grow tired of hearing them, and something in the echo shifted, and what came back to him was not his own voice at all, but the voice, some say, of his long-dead father, forgiving him for a debt Perrin had never known his father held against him. Whether Perrin ever surfaced again is a detail the tale changes depending on who tells it, but the shape of it never changes: the debt-walker goes down carrying a weight, and the maze, if it is generous, does not remove the weight but teaches the walker how to set it down without dropping it entirely.

Keepers, when apprentices ask about Perrin, tend to say only that debts brought into a drowned place have a way of behaving like water themselves, seeking always the lowest and truest point, and that a seeker who lies to himself about what he owes will find the chambers arranging themselves, quite without malice, to correct him.

### The Name-Carriers

The name-carriers are, by every seeker's account, the saddest and the most stubborn of the three companies, and also the ones whose stories are told with the greatest tenderness, because everyone understands, even the empty-handed, what it costs to walk a path for someone else's sake. The tale told most often in this vein belongs to a woman called Sael Dorn, whose brother had gone into the maze three winters before her and had not returned, and who had left behind, scratched onto a strip of oiled cloth, only the words *tell the Keeper of the Garden that I kept my promise*, with no explanation of what the promise had been.

Sael carried that scrap of cloth for three years before she went down after him, and every seeker who tells her story makes much of the fact that she never once tried to read more into the message than what it said. She did not invent a grander mission for her brother. She simply resolved to find whichever Keeper watched over the Petrified Garden and deliver seven words that were not even fully hers to deliver. The tale holds that when she finally stood among those stone-still trees, the Keeper who met her already knew the message before she spoke it, and told her, gently, that her brother had indeed kept his promise, though the nature of the promise was never disclosed to her, nor to any seeker since, and this omission is treated by tellers not as a flaw in the story but as its entire point. Sael Dorn is remembered not because she solved a mystery but because she refused to turn someone else's private vow into her own public triumph. She delivered the words and left, and to this day when a seeker sets out to finish a dead companion's unfinished business, the others will say, quietly, that they hope the seeker walks it the way Sael walked it: without embellishing another person's grief into their own legend.

### Those Who Come for Nothing

The empty-handed are harder to make into satisfying stories, because a story wants a reason, and the empty-handed have none to give, which is precisely why the Keepers are said to watch them most closely of all. The tale of Wick Farrow is told to illustrate this, usually by an older seeker to a younger one who has just admitted, shamefaced, that they cannot say why they came.

Wick Farrow had a trade, a home, a wife who loved him and did not understand him, and no debt, no promise, no grief driving him toward the drowned stair. He simply woke one morning unable to stop thinking about the maze, the way some people cannot stop thinking about a tune they cannot place. He told his wife he would return within the week. The tale, in its cruelest tellings, has him never returning at all; in its kindest tellings, he returns after eleven years having aged only a season, unable to explain the discrepancy and unwilling to try. Between those two extremes lie a hundred variations, and seekers argue about which is the true one the way people argue about the true ending to any story that matters more for what it teaches than for what actually happened.

What every version agrees on is this: Wick Farrow, asked by the first Keeper he met why he had come, answered honestly that he did not know, and the Keeper is said to have found this answer more acceptable than any lie dressed up as purpose. The moral, as it is generally delivered around the causeway fires, is that the maze does not require a seeker to justify their hunger, only to be truthful about having one, and that the emptiest hands are sometimes the most honest ones to bring through the gate.

## Tales Told at the Sunken Gate

### The Ballad of Old Corrin

Every company of seekers waiting at the gate eventually sings, or half-sings, or recites in a sing-song mutter that has forgotten it was ever meant to have a tune, the ballad of Old Corrin, who is said to have entered the maze nine times and left it nine times, an achievement so peculiar that no one agrees whether it makes him the luckiest seeker who ever lived or the unluckiest, since a person who must return nine times has plainly not yet gotten what he came for.

The ballad, rendered here as it is told rather than sung, begins with Corrin young and broke and desperate, entering the Sunken Atrium with nothing but his father's boots and a hunger he could not name, and finding, on that first descent, barely enough to matter, a trinket, a scrap of old fortune, just enough to convince him the maze was worth returning to. It continues through each of his nine journeys, and the singers linger longest on the fourth, in which Corrin is said to have wept in the Gallery of Whispers because the voices there told him something about his mother he had never wanted to know, and the seventh, in which he grew so bold from his small successes that he strode into the Emberforge Hollow as though he owned it, and paid for that pride in a way the song only alludes to, never states outright, because some humiliations are better left to the listener's imagination.

By the ninth journey, the ballad slows, and the tellers lower their voices, because this is the part meant to be believed rather than merely enjoyed. Corrin, old now, his father's boots long since worn through and replaced twice over, enters the maze not for treasure at all but, the song says, out of habit, the way an old man returns to a chair he has always sat in even after the room around it has changed beyond recognition. He does not go seeking anything. He goes because the maze has become the only place that still remembers who he was at every age he has ever been, and when he finally does not come back a tenth time, the seekers waiting at the gate do not treat it as a tragedy. They treat it as a man finally being where he belonged. The refrain of the ballad, repeated after every verse until the listeners are murmuring along without meaning to, translates roughly to: *the drowned halls keep better count of us than we keep of ourselves.*

### The Woman Who Counted the Chambers Backward

Among the tales meant more to unsettle than to comfort is that of a seeker known only as the Countess of Nothing, a nickname earned not from any noble blood but from her peculiar habit of announcing, before she entered the maze, that she intended to visit its eight chambers in reverse order from whatever order fate proposed to give her. Seekers telling this story always pause here to note, with a kind of relish, that no one has ever explained how a person could choose such a thing, since the maze is famous for delivering its chambers to a seeker according to a logic no visitor has ever mastered or predicted. And yet the Countess insisted she would do it, loudly, to anyone who would listen, in the days before her descent.

She is said to have vanished into the Mercury Spring first, which the tale treats as either an impossible coincidence or a sign that she had, in fact, found some private understanding with the maze that she shared with no one. From there the story grows stranger with each telling: she is glimpsed, always briefly and always by seekers who admit they might have imagined it, moving through the Whisperbone Catacomb, then the Starfall Observatory, then backward still through chambers she should not, by any sane telling, have been able to choose. The story never resolves. No one claims to know whether she completed her backward circuit or whether the maze, tolerant of many things, drew a quiet line at being told its own order by a visitor.

Keepers, on the rare occasions they are asked directly about the Countess, neither confirm nor deny that such a seeker ever existed, which seekers take, naturally, as confirmation. The tale is told less as history and more as a kind of dare, whispered to bold young seekers who talk too confidently about mastering the maze's patterns: *go on, then, count it backward, and see how far the drowned halls let you get before they remind you whose house you are standing in.*

### The Seeker of the Ninth Return

A gentler tale, often told to calm a nervous first-timer, concerns a boy named Tam Ovett, no older than fifteen winters when he first came to the gate, too young by every custom to descend alone, and turned away three times by the Keepers before he was ever let past the threshold. Tam's story is beloved not for what he found in the maze but for what he did while waiting to be allowed in: he spent two years camped at the causeway, running errands for older seekers, learning the etiquette of the gate, memorizing which offerings were welcome and which were merely superstition dressed up as ritual, until by the time a Keeper finally judged him ready, he was, by every seeker's account, more fluent in the culture of the maze than visitors three times his age.

The tale makes much of Tam's patience, holding it up as proof that the maze rewards those who understand it is not a door to be kicked down but a household to be entered properly, as a guest enters a home whose customs are not their own. When Tam finally descended, the story goes, he moved through the chambers with a calm that unsettled even seasoned seekers who heard of it afterward, not because he found extraordinary fortune but because he seemed to expect nothing and was grateful for whatever came, which the tellers insist is the truest sign of a seeker who has learned the place rather than merely visited it. Parents bringing their own children to the gate for the first time still invoke Tam Ovett by name, less as a hero to imitate in deed than as a model of temperament: wait, watch, learn the shape of the silence before you ever expect the silence to answer you.

## Famous Seekers and Their Wanderings

### Brannoc Half-Lit

No name carries more weight among seekers, nor more caution, than that of Brannoc Half-Lit, so called because of how his tale always ends, half in triumph and half in ruin, and no teller has ever managed to satisfy an audience that one half without the other. Brannoc was, by every account, the boldest seeker of his generation, a man who treated the eight chambers the way other men treat familiar rooms in their own house, striding through the Sunken Atrium and into deeper halls with a confidence that younger seekers found intoxicating and older ones found faintly terrifying to witness.

His most famous wandering took him, the story says, through five chambers in what the tellers describe only as a single unbroken venture, an achievement so far beyond ordinary seeking that some doubt any of it happened as told, and yet the tale persists precisely because its ending refuses to let it be dismissed as mere boast. In the fifth chamber, whichever one the teller favors that night, Brannoc is said to have grown careless, not from exhaustion but from the particular arrogance that comes from having been right too many times in a row. He pressed on when a wiser seeker would have turned back, certain that whatever had favored him so far would continue to favor him, and it is here that every version of the tale grows quiet and careful, because what happened to Brannoc in that final chamber is never described directly. He is simply said to have emerged from the maze forever changed, alive, yes, and even in possession of some prize worth the telling, but altered in a way that made every seeker who met him afterward lower their voice in his presence, as though something about him had been left behind in the drowned dark and something else had been brought back in its place.

The lesson drawn from Brannoc, delivered most often to seekers who have just enjoyed a run of good fortune, is not that boldness is wrong. It is that boldness untethered from humility eventually collects its own price, and that the maze, generous as it can be to the confident, does not forget to remind the confident that confidence is not the same as safety. Old seekers say that if you meet a stranger at the gate who moves like Brannoc did in his prime, you should wish them well and then, quietly, wish for their own sake that they remember to be afraid at least once before they are done.

### The Sisters of the Mercury Spring

A gentler and more hopeful tale belongs to two sisters, Ovena and Ress Calloway, who are remembered less for treasure than for the manner of their seeking, which every teller holds up as an example of how companionship can carry a person through places that would break someone walking alone. The sisters entered the maze together, an arrangement so unusual that the gatekeepers of their day are said to have argued over whether it should even be permitted, since seeking is understood by nearly everyone to be a solitary undertaking, a reckoning between one person and the drowned dark that no companion can share.

The sisters, however, insisted that their bond was itself a kind of offering, that they had never in their lives faced anything separately and did not intend to start with the maze. What happened to them afterward became one of the most cherished tales told to seekers who fear the loneliness of the descent more than they fear anything the chambers might contain. In the Mercury Spring, the story goes, the sisters found themselves separated despite every effort to stay together, each believing the other lost, each grieving in isolation for a companion who was, in truth, only a chamber away. The tale's tenderness lies in what happened next: rather than pressing forward in search of prize or exit, both sisters are said to have stopped where they stood and called out, over and over, patient and unhurried, trusting that if their bond meant anything at all it would find a way to answer itself. And it did, eventually, the story insists, though no teller agrees on how long *eventually* took, and the sisters emerged from the maze together, changed by the fear of losing each other more than by anything they had found.

Seekers who travel with kin or dear friends still invoke the sisters before a descent, less as instruction than as blessing, a way of saying: may you be separated only long enough to learn what your companion means to you, and never longer than that.

### Ilsevet Who Never Asked

The most curious of the famous seekers, and the one whose tale is told with the most discomfort, is a woman known simply as Ilsevet, who is remembered not for anything she found in the maze but for the fact that she is said never, in the entirety of her single long descent, to have spoken a single word to a Keeper. Every other seeker's tale, no matter how it ends, includes at least one exchange with a Keeper, a request made and either granted or refused, a question asked and answered or left hanging. Ilsevet's tale contains no such moment, and this absence is precisely what makes her famous.

The tellers disagree on why she kept her silence. Some say she believed speaking to a Keeper would somehow diminish whatever she was seeking, as though the asking itself were a kind of payment she was unwilling to make. Others say she was simply too proud, or too frightened, to open a conversation with something so plainly beyond her understanding. A few, and this is the version favored by Keepers themselves when they deign to comment on the tale at all, suggest that Ilsevet mistook silence for humility, when in truth a seeker who never asks anything of a Keeper is not being humble at all, only isolating herself from the one relationship in the maze capable of guiding her.

Whatever the reason, the tale ends without triumph and without disaster, which unsettles listeners more than either extreme would. Ilsevet is simply said to have wandered, chamber after chamber, achieving neither ruin nor reward, and to have left the maze eventually with the peculiar hollow look of someone who suspects she missed something she will never be able to name. Apprentice Keepers are told this story specifically to explain why a Keeper does not wait passively for a seeker to speak, but this same telling is repeated to seekers with the opposite lesson attached: that the courtesy of asking exists for the asker's own sake as much as anyone else's, and that silence, in a place built on exchange, is not caution but a kind of self-inflicted wound.

## The Etiquette of Asking a Keeper

There is no written code for how a seeker ought to address a Warden-Keeper, and yet every seeker who has spent even a season among the others waiting at the gate absorbs the same customs so thoroughly that they come to feel less like manners and more like instinct. The first and most repeated of these is that a seeker should never demand. This sounds obvious stated plainly, but the tales exist precisely because it is not obvious in practice, not to a person standing exhausted and frightened before a figure who seems to hold every answer they have traveled so far to hear. The temptation to demand, to insist, to treat the Keeper as an obstacle rather than a host, is said to visit every seeker at least once, and the ones who succumb to it are the subjects of a hundred cautionary tales, most of which end with the Keeper simply turning away, unmoved, leaving the demanding seeker no worse off than before but no better off either, which every teller agrees is its own kind of quiet punishment.

The second custom, nearly as old, is that a question asked of a Keeper should be asked plainly, without dressing it up in flattery or false humility, both of which Keepers are said to see through instantly and find, if anything, faintly wearying. Seekers new to the gate often arrive having rehearsed elaborate speeches, full of praise for the maze and its guardians, believing that eloquence will earn favor. The old hands know better and tell the story of a seeker named Dessa Vray to prove it, a woman so practiced in flattering speech that she could reportedly charm a debt collector into an apology, who arrived before a Keeper and delivered a paragraph of praise so ornate that the Keeper, according to every telling, waited until she finished and then asked her, without any apparent irritation, whether she intended to ask her question before nightfall. Dessa is remembered fondly rather than mockingly, because the tale is not really about her failure, it is about the lesson she carried afterward and shared freely: a Keeper wants to know what you seek, not how well you can speak of seeking it.

The third custom, and the one seekers argue about most, concerns the small offerings left at the gate before a descent, tokens of copper or carved bone or a lock of hair, gestures that every seeker insists matter enormously and that every Keeper, when pressed, describes as unnecessary, which does nothing at all to stop seekers from leaving them. The tale usually cited here belongs to an old woman called Merrow Fenn, who left nothing at the gate at all, not from stinginess but because she believed, and said so to anyone who would listen, that a Keeper's attention could not be purchased and should not be treated as though it could. Merrow's descent, the tale reports, went no better and no worse than those of seekers who arrived laden with tokens, which the tellers hold up as proof that the offerings are a comfort to the giver rather than a currency accepted by the receiver, though this has never once stopped a nervous seeker from tying some small thing to the gate rail before stepping through, because superstition, unlike etiquette, does not require proof to persist.

The final and most solemn custom is that a seeker, once granted an answer or a passage or whatever small mercy a Keeper chooses to extend, should thank the Keeper and then say nothing further, resisting the urge to ask for more. This is the custom most often broken, and the tales that punish its breaking are the harshest of all, because greed dressed as gratitude is, in the telling, the ugliest disguise greed ever wears. A seeker who has just been given something precious and immediately asks for a second favor is treated, in every version of every relevant story, as having undone the grace of the first gift entirely, regardless of whether the Keeper grants the second request or refuses it. The asking itself is the transgression. Old seekers teach this to the young with a phrase that has become something between a proverb and a warning, repeated so often at the gate that it has lost its original teller entirely: *one hand open, one hand still.*

## Superstitions of the Causeway

Long before a seeker ever crosses into the first chamber, they have already absorbed a hundred small superstitions from the company waiting at the gate, most of which contradict each other and none of which any seeker will admit to taking seriously, right up until the moment they perform the ritual anyway. It is said, for instance, that a seeker should never speak their own name aloud while standing on the entry stair, because the drowned water carries sound strangely and there are those who believe something down in the dark collects names the way a magpie collects bright things, and having your name known before you have earned the right to be known is thought to invite an attention no seeker actually wants. No one can say where this belief began, and Keepers, when asked, neither confirm nor mock it, which seekers take, as they take most Keeper silences, as a kind of confirmation.

Another superstition holds that a seeker should carry something old into the maze rather than something new, a worn coin rather than a freshly minted one, a coat handed down rather than bought for the occasion, on the reasoning that the drowned halls are themselves ancient beyond telling and are said to look more kindly on things that have already survived some portion of time. This belief has given rise to a small, quiet trade at the edges of the causeway, where seekers who have decided not to descend, or who have returned and no longer need their gear, pass their worn belongings on to newcomers rather than let them go to waste, a practice that began as superstition and has become, almost incidentally, one of the few genuine kindnesses the seeker community extends to its newest members.

There is a belief, too, about the failing of a torch, spoken of by seekers in hushed and careful tones, not as a mechanical event but as an omen heavy with meaning. A torch that dims unexpectedly, seekers say, is not merely bad fortune but a message, though what message depends entirely on who is doing the interpreting. Some hold that a failing torch means the maze is asking the seeker to slow down, to reconsider whatever haste has brought them to that point. Others insist it means precisely the opposite, that the dark is testing whether the seeker's nerve will fail alongside the flame, and that pressing onward through the dimming light is the only respectable response. Because the two interpretations demand opposite actions, seekers have never resolved which is correct, and the argument over what a failing torch truly means has been carried on, essentially unchanged, for as long as anyone can remember, passed down not as wisdom but as an enduring and almost comfortable disagreement, the kind of argument old friends have not because they hope to win it but because having it is itself a form of company against the dark.

A final superstition, gentler than the rest, holds that a seeker who hums while walking the Gallery of Whispers will find the voices there kinder, on the theory that the chamber, whatever else it may be, appreciates being sung to rather than merely listened to in silence. No seeker can explain why this should be true, and most admit, if pressed honestly, that they hum there simply because it steadies their own nerves rather than because they believe the chamber notices or cares. But belief, among seekers, has never required certainty, only usefulness, and a superstition that calms a frightened traveler is rarely abandoned merely for lacking proof.

## Parables the Keepers Tell Their Apprentices

### On Patience: The Snail in the Vault of Echoes

Among the oldest teachings passed from senior Keeper to apprentice is the parable of the snail, told in almost identical words across generations of Keepers, which itself is treated as evidence of the story's importance, since Keepers are not given to preserving things without cause. The parable tells of a snail who found itself, through no ambition of its own, deposited upon the floor of the Vault of Echoes, and who resolved, in its small and patient way, to cross the entire chamber from one end to the other, though it could not have said why this crossing mattered or what waited on the far side.

The snail moved, as snails do, with a slowness that would try the patience of any creature capable of impatience, and the chamber, being what it is, returned every small sound of its passage back upon it, amplified and strange, so that the snail seemed to hear itself moving from every direction at once, a chorus of its own tiny effort surrounding it on all sides. A hare, so the parable continues, happened into the chamber during this crossing, and mocked the snail for the absurdity of its ambition, pointing out that the hare could cross the same distance in a single afternoon and have time left over to nap. The snail did not argue. It simply continued moving, at the only pace it had ever known how to move, and the parable notes, with the particular relish Keepers reserve for morals they consider especially instructive, that the hare grew so entertained by its own mockery that it stayed in the chamber far longer than it intended, distracted by the echo of its own laughter bouncing back to it in increasingly strange forms, until the hare could no longer tell which laugh was its own and which was merely the chamber returning an old one to it. By the time the hare recovered itself enough to continue on its way, the snail, moving at the only speed available to it, had already reached the far wall.

Keepers tell this to apprentices not to suggest that slowness is inherently virtuous, since even Keepers admit that a snail's pace is nothing to aspire to for its own sake, but to illustrate that a creature who moves according to its own true nature, undistracted by the noise its efforts generate, will always outlast one who becomes entranced by the sound of its own cleverness. Seekers, the apprentices are told, come in both varieties, and a Keeper's task is not to hurry the snails or humble the hares, but simply to recognize which is which, and to treat both with the same unhurried patience the chamber itself extends to any creature willing to keep moving.

### On Greed: The Man Who Filled Both Hands

The parable told most often on the subject of greed concerns a seeker, unnamed in every version because the Keepers who tell it insist the story is meant to belong to whoever hears it rather than to any single historical figure, who came upon something precious within the maze and found that he could carry it easily in one hand, with the other left free for whatever else the day might offer. Satisfied, he continued onward, and soon found something else, equally precious, which he took into his free hand as well, and here the parable pauses to note, with a plainness that apprentices are told to notice precisely because it seems so unremarkable at first hearing, that the man now had both hands full and nothing left free at all.

He continued deeper regardless, the parable says, because having two full hands had not diminished his hunger for a third find, only made it impossible to act on that hunger honestly. When he came upon a narrow passage that required him to steady himself against the wall with one hand to keep from falling into the dark below, he found he could not do it, both hands being occupied, and so he attempted the passage anyway, trusting to balance alone, and lost his footing, and in catching himself instinctively against the wall, dropped one of the two things he had been so careful to carry, watching it vanish into whatever lay beneath the passage before he had even finished catching his breath. The parable is careful to note that he did not lose both. He kept one. But the story does not treat this as a small mercy so much as a bitter joke, since the man is said to have spent the remainder of his journey mourning the one thing he lost far more intensely than he ever celebrated the one thing he kept, his grief for the dropped prize entirely swallowing his gratitude for the held one.

Keepers close this parable with a line so often repeated that apprentices learn to recite it before they fully understand it: a hand that is never opened cannot be filled, but a hand that is never emptied cannot be trusted to hold what matters most. The lesson, as it is explained afterward to apprentices still puzzling over the phrase, is not that seekers should refuse whatever the maze offers them, but that greed announces itself less in the wanting than in the refusal to ever set anything down, and that a seeker who cannot bear to leave one hand free for their own safety has already lost more than whatever the dark eventually takes from them.

### On Curses: The Bell That Rang Once

Perhaps the most feared of all the parables, reserved for apprentices who have already spent enough seasons among the Keepers to be trusted with something unsettling, is the tale of the bell that rang once, said to hang, in whatever half-real way the maze permits such things to hang, somewhere within the Whisperbone Catacomb, though no living Keeper claims to have seen it directly. The parable holds that the bell was cursed, though the nature of the curse is never described in terms an apprentice could act upon or even fully picture, only felt, the way the parable is told, as a kind of dread that sits behind the words rather than within them.

A seeker, the tale goes, found the bell and, hearing that it was cursed, resolved immediately never to touch it, and spent the remainder of his time in that chamber giving it as wide a berth as the narrow passages allowed, so consumed by his effort to avoid the thing that he saw almost nothing else the chamber had to offer, walking past wonders with his eyes fixed sideways on the bell the entire time, as though it might leap upon him the instant his attention wavered. Another seeker, arriving some time later according to the parable's telling, heard the same warning and reacted in precisely the opposite manner, striding directly to the bell and striking it deliberately, on the theory, as she explained to no one in particular since she was alone, that a curse faced head-on loses whatever power it holds over those who fear it in secret.

The parable, and this is the detail Keepers dwell on longest when explaining it to apprentices, refuses to say which seeker fared better. Both are described only as having left the chamber, alive, seemingly unchanged, and the tale offers no verdict on which approach, terrified avoidance or deliberate confrontation, proved wiser. This absence of a clear moral is, apprentices are told, the entire point of the parable, and the one that troubles young Keepers most until they have sat with it long enough. Curses, the senior Keepers explain, are not obstacles with a single correct response the way a locked door has a single correct key. A curse is a kind of question the maze asks a seeker about themselves, and the answer that serves one seeker rightly may ruin another entirely, and a Keeper's duty is never to tell a seeker whether to run from the bell or ring it, only to make certain the seeker understands, before they choose, that the choosing itself is the whole of what will be asked of them.

### On the Failing of the Torch: The Keeper Who Wept

The gentlest and, according to many apprentices, the most affecting of all the parables is also the newest, at least in the sense that no one claims it comes from the deep antiquity the other tales are wrapped in. It concerns a Keeper, not a seeker, which alone marks it as unusual among the teaching tales, since most parables are built around the failings and virtues of visitors rather than of the Keepers themselves. This Keeper, the story goes, had tended her portion of the maze for so long that she had come to know, or believed she had come to know, every seeker who might ever pass beneath her care, and had grown, without quite noticing the change in herself, weary of watching torches gutter and fail in the hands of travelers who could not always be saved from the consequences of their own dimming light.

One evening, if evening can be said to mean anything in a place the surface sun never reaches, a young seeker came before her whose torch had failed almost as soon as she had entered the chamber, through no fault the Keeper could identify, neither carelessness nor greed nor any of the usual causes the other parables warn against. The girl simply stood in the encroaching dark, frightened but not panicked, and asked the Keeper, plainly and without flattery, exactly as the old etiquette instructs, whether there was any hope left for her at all. The parable says the Keeper found herself, for the first time in longer than she could remember, unable to answer immediately, because she realized in that moment that she had grown so accustomed to watching torches fail that she had stopped feeling the weight of what a failing torch actually meant to the person holding it, had begun treating each failure as merely another instance of a pattern she had already catalogued a thousand times before rather than as the singular catastrophe it was to the girl standing in front of her.

The Keeper wept, the parable says, not loudly and not for long, but genuinely, and this weeping is the detail every teller lingers on, because a weeping Keeper is so contrary to how seekers imagine the Warden-Keepers that the detail alone tends to silence a room of apprentices the first time they hear it. She wept, the story explains, not because she believed her tears could relight the girl's torch, and not out of despair that nothing could be done, but simply because she had, for one unguarded moment, allowed herself to feel the full weight of what it means to stand in failing light without pretending the feeling was beneath her notice. And it was this, the parable insists, this refusal to let long familiarity curdle into indifference, that was the true teaching, more than whatever became of the girl and her torch afterward, which the story treats almost as an afterthought.

Senior Keepers tell this parable to apprentices specifically at the point in their training when the work has begun to feel routine, when the wonder of the drowned halls has started to flatten under the weight of repetition, because they know, better than anyone, how easily a Keeper's compassion can calcify into procedure if it is not tended as carefully as any torch. A Keeper who no longer feels the weight of a failing light, the old ones say, has failed more thoroughly than any seeker ever could, and the parable exists to make certain no apprentice forgets it, no matter how many seasons of seekers pass beneath their care.

## The Songs Sung at the Gate

Beyond the tales and the parables, the culture of the seekers lives in its songs, though almost none of them are written down, surviving instead the way all such things survive among people who spend long stretches of time together waiting for something uncertain: through repetition, through the correction of an elder singer when a younger one gets a verse wrong, through the simple pleasure of a shared tune in a place that offers little else in the way of comfort.

The song called the Waterline Round is perhaps the most beloved, sung not for any particular occasion but simply whenever a company of seekers finds itself gathered at the causeway with nothing to do but wait, its verses passed between singers in a rotating pattern, each new voice adding a line about a chamber they have heard of but never seen, so that the song grows differently depending on who happens to be present that night, some verses praising the strange stillness of the Petrified Garden, others describing the endless stars said to wheel overhead in the Starfall Observatory in defiance of the fact that the whole vault lies drowned beneath water that has not seen open sky in an age. No two tellings of the Waterline Round are identical, and seekers consider this not a flaw but the entire virtue of the song, since a maze that reshuffles its own halls deserves, they say, a song that reshuffles itself in turn.

There is also a quieter song, sung less often and usually only among seekers who already know each other well, called the Leaving Verse, meant to be sung not before a descent but after one, by those who return, as a way of marking the fact that they have come back changed in ways they may never fully explain to those who did not go down with them. The Leaving Verse has no fixed tune that any two seekers agree on, and this too is treated as fitting, since every seeker's return is different, and a song that insisted on a single melody would betray the very truth it exists to honor. What remains constant across every version is a single repeated phrase, murmured rather than sung outright, that translates roughly to: *I left something down there, and I do not want it back.* Seekers who have never descended find this phrase strange, even troubling, but those who have made the journey nod along without needing it explained, understanding instinctively that every true seeking costs something the seeker is glad, in the end, to have paid.

A third song, more mischievous than the other two, is reserved for teasing the boastful, sung pointedly whenever a seeker returns from the maze with a story that grows visibly larger with each retelling. Called simply the Big Fish Round by most who sing it, it borrows its structure from old fishing songs and applies the same gentle mockery to any seeker who claims to have wrestled some impossible peril in the Emberforge Hollow or stared down a curse in the Whisperbone Catacomb without so much as a tremor. The song does not accuse the boaster of lying outright, which would be considered unkind, but instead exaggerates the claim even further, adding absurd flourishes verse by verse until the whole company is laughing, including, more often than not, the very seeker being teased, who understands the ritual for what it is: a community's gentle way of keeping its members honest without ever needing to call anyone a liar to their face.

## The Argument of Old and New Seekers

No account of seeker culture would be complete without mentioning the long, good-natured argument that runs beneath nearly every conversation at the causeway between those who have descended many times and those preparing for their first journey, an argument that never resolves and, seekers generally agree, is not meant to. The old seekers insist that the maze rewards familiarity, that a seeker who has learned its moods across many visits carries an advantage no amount of raw courage can replace. The newcomers, for their part, insist just as fervently that the maze favors fresh eyes, unclouded by the accumulated assumptions of prior visits, and that a seeker who thinks they already understand the place is the very seeker most likely to be caught unprepared by whatever the drowned halls choose to show them next.

Both camps can produce a dozen tales in support of their position, and both camps happily ignore the dozen tales that would undermine it, which the elders among them consider not hypocrisy but simply the natural way any community holds onto the beliefs it finds most useful for its own courage. What matters, the wisest of the old seekers will admit if pressed late enough into an evening and deep enough into a shared skin of wine, is not which side is correct but that the argument itself gives newcomers something to hold onto besides fear, a debate to join rather than a silence to sit in, and gives the old hands a reason to keep returning to the causeway even in seasons when they have no intention of descending again themselves, simply to keep the argument, and the company that comes with it, alive.

## The Names Left at the Gate

There is one final custom, less a superstition than a quiet grief made communal, that binds the seeker culture together more than any tale or song. At the mouth of the causeway, where the entry stair first meets open air, generations of seekers have taken to scratching names into the stone, not their own names but the names of those who went down and never came back, a wall of small marks accumulated over so long a span that no one now living can say which names belong to seekers whose stories are still told and which belong to seekers so long forgotten that only the mark itself remains, its story lost entirely to time.

New seekers are taught, almost as soon as they arrive at the gate, to read a few of these names aloud before their first descent, not because doing so is believed to grant any particular fortune, but because the old hands consider it a matter of basic respect, a way of carrying the company of the dead alongside the company of the living into whatever waits below. It is said, though no one can say by whom it was first said, that a seeker who reads the old names honestly, without hurry and without performance, walks into the maze a little less alone than a seeker who skips past the wall without a glance, and whether or not this is true in any way a Keeper would recognize, it has become, over the long slow accumulation of years, simply what it means to be a seeker at all: to add your own hunger to a wall already carrying the hungers of everyone who came before you, and to trust, the way every seeker before you has had to trust, that the drowned dark below remembers kindness even when it remembers little else.
# Part Two, Book V - The Fall of the Drowned World

## Chapter One: The Age of Bright Cities

Before the waters closed over the world the way a lid closes over a jar of preserved fruit, there was a age that its own people called, without irony, the Standing Age. They believed the ground beneath their feet had stood since the beginning of time and would stand until its end, and they built accordingly, in stone and bronze and the strange glassy alloys their forges alone knew how to temper. The heart of that age was the Aurelian Concord, a confederation of city-states bound not by a single crown but by a shared reverence for measurement, memory, and the slow accumulation of knowledge against the erosions of forgetting. Where other peoples of that distant time raised temples to gods of thunder or harvest, the Concord raised archives, observatories, and forges, and called these its temples, and called its scholars and engineers something very near to priests.

The Concord's capital was Vessantine, a city built upon seven terraces descending to a wide estuary where three rivers met the sea in a slow, brackish confluence the old surveyors named the Threefold Mouth. From its high terraces one could see, on a clear evening, the lamplit spires of Thessaly Deep to the south and the smoke of the forge-city of Korrhaven rising from the eastern hills, and it was said that a Vessantine child, looking out at those distant lights, understood before she understood anything else that she belonged to a world stitched together by roads, canals, and the tireless correspondence of scholars. The Concord's other great settlements ringed the inland sea like beads on a strand: Nimbral Reach with its terraced vineyards and its famous school of horologists, who built clocks that measured the drift of stars as patiently as the passage of hours; the Salt Quarters, a sprawling confederation of harbor towns that fed and clothed the whole Concord from its fisheries and its dye-works; and, far to the north, half-legendary even in its own time, the mining city of Kesh-Undertow, which burrowed so deep into the earth's crust that its miners joked they would one day meet the miners of the antipodes coming up from the other side.

It was a civilization proud of its patience. The Concord's founding charter, inscribed on a slab of black basalt that stood in the Vessantine forum for four centuries, declared that no work worth doing could be finished within a single lifetime, and that the mark of a civilized people was its willingness to labor for grandchildren it would never meet. This was not empty rhetoric. The great canal that linked the Threefold Mouth to the inland sea took ninety years to dig and was completed by the great-grandchildren of the engineers who broke its first ground. The Concord's calendar itself, a marvel of interlocking cycles that reconciled the drift of the moon with the slower wobble of the earth's axis, had been refined across eleven generations of astronomers before it was declared, with considerable ceremony, complete. There is a kind of civilization that measures its greatness in conquest, and there is a kind that measures it in the patience of its unfinished projects, handed down like heirlooms from the dying to the newborn. The Aurelian Concord was unmistakably the second kind, and for nearly six hundred years by its own reckoning, that patience served it well.

Wealth in the Concord was not gold, though gold circulated freely enough in its markets. Wealth was information: a well-kept ledger, a verified star-chart, a seed catalogued and stored against blight, a song transcribed before its last singer died. The Concord's guilds competed with one another not for territory but for the completeness of their archives, and a guild's standing among its peers was measured by how much of the world it had managed to write down, preserve, and cross-reference before some rival guild claimed the same ground. This obsession with preservation, later generations would say, was both the Concord's genius and the seed of its undoing, for a people so devoted to keeping what they had would prove, when the moment came, catastrophically reluctant to let any of it go.

The engineers of the Concord were called Wrights, and among the Wrights the highest honor was to be named to the Cartographers of Depth, a guild that mapped not roads or coastlines but the substrata of the earth itself: aquifers, fault lines, the slow migrations of groundwater through limestone over centuries. It was the Cartographers of Depth who first understood, long before anyone else in the Concord was willing to listen, that the ground beneath Vessantine's seven terraces was not the changeless bedrock its people imagined it to be, but something closer to a held breath.

The Concord kept no single faith in the way its neighboring peoples did, but it kept something that served the same purpose in its citizens' hearts: a civic reverence for what it called the Long Accounting, the belief that every generation stood as a debtor and a creditor at once, owing something to the dead who had built its roads and libraries, and owed something in turn by the unborn who would inherit whatever was left unfinished. Public festivals throughout the Concord's calendar were built around this idea rather than around any god's favor; the greatest of them, the Feast of Unfinished Work held each year in every city-state on the same appointed night, saw citizens gather to read aloud from the letters and ledgers of ancestors three and four generations gone, a practice that bound the Concord's scattered cities together in shared memory even when their politics quarreled. It was a beautiful custom, and it produced, without anyone quite intending it, a civilization exquisitely equipped to remember its past and poorly equipped to imagine an ending to its future, a flaw that would not become visible until it was very nearly too late to matter.

## Chapter Two: The Guilds of Engineers and Archivists

Two institutions stood at the center of Concord life, and their long partnership, and eventual rupture, shaped everything that followed. The first was the Wrights' Concordance, the guild of engineers, founded some three hundred years before the flood by a coalition of shipbuilders, canal-diggers, and mining-masters who had grown weary of each city-state guarding its techniques as private treasure. The Wrights' great innovation was not any single invention but the principle of shared method: that a technique proven in Korrhaven's forges should be written down, tested, and taught in Nimbral Reach's workshops within a single season, rather than rediscovered independently a generation later. Under this principle the Wrights raised aqueducts that ran for a hundred miles without a single failed arch, built the great tide-gates that regulated the Threefold Mouth's harbor, and eventually turned their attention to a project so ambitious that even their own guild-masters debated for a decade whether it should be attempted at all: a chambered vault, sunk deep into the bedrock beneath Vessantine, meant to outlast the city itself.

The second institution was the Archive Sable, sometimes called the Sable Curia, a body of scholars, scribes, and memory-keepers whose founding predated even the Concord's political union. Where the Wrights built with stone and bronze, the Archivists built with parchment, wax tablet, and eventually the strange crystalline lattices their chemists learned to etch with a stylus of diamond dust, a medium the Archivists believed would outlast paper by ten thousand years. The Sable Curia's High Archivist held a seat on the Concord's ruling council equal to any city's magistrate, a testament to how deeply the Concord had come to believe that a civilization's memory was as vital to its survival as its granaries or its garrisons.

For most of the Concord's history the Wrights and the Archivists worked as uneasy but productive partners: the engineers built the vaults, aqueducts, and observatories; the archivists filled them with catalogued knowledge and argued, sometimes for years, over what deserved preservation and what could be permitted to fade. It was a partnership formalized, near the end of the Concord's third century, in the founding charter of what would become the great work of both guilds together: the sunken repository beneath Vessantine that the Wrights called the Deephold and the Archivists, with their fondness for grander names, called the Ninefold Ark, though it would in the end hold only eight chambers, the ninth having been abandoned half-dug when the bedrock beneath it proved treacherous.

The Deephold was not, in its earliest conception, built against any flood. It was built as an act of pure civilizational vanity and pure civilizational wisdom in equal measure: a place where the Concord's accumulated knowledge, art, seed-stock, and the remains of its honored dead might be kept safe against fire, war, or the ordinary decay of centuries, so that whatever came after the Concord, whether its own distant descendants or some stranger people entirely, would inherit something more than rubble. Its eight chambers were carved and raised across nearly a hundred years, each entrusted to a different guild and given a different purpose. The Sunken Atrium, nearest the surface, was built as the great processional entrance, its vaulted ceiling painted with a mural of the Concord's founding that no living eye would see finished, since the muralists who began it were dead of old age before the last figure was painted in. Deeper down, the Gallery of Whispers was built by the Archivists as a repository for the Concord's art, its literature, and, more strangely, recordings of voices: the Sable Curia had developed a technique for etching sound itself into their crystal lattices, and the Gallery was filled, chamber by chamber, with the recorded voices of poets, statesmen, and ordinary citizens who wished some trace of themselves to outlast their bodies.

Emberforge Hollow, lower still, was the Wrights' own province, a chamber of forges and workshops kept perpetually warm by channels drawing heat from deeper strata, where apprentice engineers might, in some imagined future, relearn the guild's crafts should the surface world's knowledge ever be lost. The Vault of Echoes, built adjacent to it, held the Concord's technical archive proper: its engineering diagrams, its mathematics, its star-charts, guarded as jealously as the Gallery guarded its art. The Petrified Garden, an underground chamber flooded with captured daylight through ingenious mirrored shafts, held the Concord's seed vaults and its botanical gardens, tended by a small hereditary order of gardener-priests who believed, with a conviction the rest of the Concord found faintly absurd, that plants remembered things soil forgot. The Starfall Observatory, cut into the highest and driest reach of the complex, housed instruments for tracking the heavens even from within the earth, a project the horologists of Nimbral Reach considered their crowning achievement. The Whisperbone Catacomb, deepest and coldest of the eight, was given over to the dead: the honored bones of the Concord's magistrates, archivists, and Wright-masters, laid in niches alongside grave-goods and inscriptions, in the belief that even death deserved a memory kept rather than a memory lost. And at the very lowest reach, where a natural spring of quicksilver bled slowly from a fissure the Cartographers of Depth never fully explained, the Mercury Spring was built as much to contain a mystery as to use one, for the strange liquid metal was found to preserve certain materials against decay in ways no one in the Concord could satisfactorily explain, and so a chamber was raised around it rather than around any purpose the Wrights could name.

It took the guilds nearly a century to complete this work, and by the time the last stone was set in the Sunken Atrium's threshold, none of the original masters who had proposed the project were still living. Their names, at least, were kept: Ostrach Vale, first Grand Wright of the Deephold, and Sennovyra Thal, the High Archivist who argued for the Gallery of Whispers against colleagues who thought recording ordinary voices a frivolous waste of crystal. Their successors inherited a marvel and, though none of them yet knew it, a shelter.

## Chapter Three: The Long Warnings

The first warning came, as warnings so often do, from someone the Concord was disposed not to believe. Her name was Amsel Korr, and she held no seat on any guild council; she was a tide-reader, one of a modest hereditary order of harbor-folk in the Salt Quarters whose traditional craft was reading the moods of the sea for fishing fleets, a skill regarded by the great guilds of Vessantine as folk practice rather than true knowledge. Korr had spent forty years recording the height of the tide against a stone pillar sunk into the harbor floor of her home town, a practice her mother and grandmother had kept before her, and in the last decade of her life she began to insist, with an urgency her neighbors found tiresome, that the sea was rising faster than the old records said it should, and that the rate of its rising was itself increasing year on year in a way no ordinary tidal cycle could explain.

Korr petitioned the Concord's ruling council three times. The first petition was received politely and filed, unread by anyone of consequence, in the Vault of Echoes' outer archive, on the reasoning that all petitions from citizens deserved preservation even when they did not deserve action. The second, presented in person before the council some six years later, was met with genuine scholarly interest from a handful of younger Cartographers of Depth, who found her data intriguing enough to request an official survey, though the survey was delayed for years by disputes over which guild should fund it. The third petition, delivered when Korr was an old woman and nearly blind, was the one that finally provoked debate, because by then two other, independent observers had begun to report similar findings: the horologists of Nimbral Reach noted that their instruments for measuring the moon's influence on the tides no longer matched the tides they actually observed, and the harbor-masters of Thessaly Deep reported that certain quays, built to accommodate the highest tides recorded in living memory, had begun to flood on ordinary days.

By the time the Cartographers of Depth completed their long-delayed survey, nearly thirty years after Korr's first petition, the truth they returned to the council was worse than anyone outside their guild had feared. The survey's chief author, a meticulous and famously unpopular Cartographer named Ilyon Draeve, presented findings suggesting that the crust beneath the entire inland sea basin was slowly subsiding, that vast reservoirs of groundwater once locked in deep aquifers were migrating and rising through channels the Concord's own centuries of mining and canal-digging had inadvertently opened, and that the polar ice fields, which the Concord's astronomers had observed for generations without much concern, were retreating at a pace with no precedent in any record the Sable Curia possessed. Draeve did not merely warn that the sea would rise. He warned, in language the council found so extreme that several magistrates walked out of the reading, that within the lifetimes of children already born, the Threefold Mouth would swallow Vessantine's lower terraces, and that within a few centuries more, the entire basin the Concord called home might lie beneath open water.

What followed was not disbelief so much as a peculiar and very human kind of deferral. The council did not deny Draeve's data; the Concord was too devoted to evidence to simply dismiss a properly conducted survey. Instead it commissioned further surveys, formed committees, and funded decades of additional study, all of which confirmed Draeve's findings with steadily diminishing uncertainty, while the practical response to those findings remained, year after year, a matter of further study rather than action. Some of this was ordinary institutional inertia, the kind that afflicts any body of people asked to spend present wealth against a future none of them will personally witness. But some of it, later chroniclers believed, was something closer to grief avoided by postponement: the Concord had built its entire identity on the premise of patient, multi-generational labor toward a stable future, and the idea that its cities themselves might not have a future to labor toward was, for many of its citizens, not merely inconvenient but unthinkable in the most literal sense, a thought their whole culture had no framework to hold.

Draeve, unlike Korr, did have standing within the guilds, and he spent the last three decades of his working life pressing the Wrights' Concordance to act on his findings even as the council dithered. It was Draeve, more than any other single figure, who first proposed repurposing the Deephold, then still primarily a repository of art and memory, into something closer to a refuge: a place whose eight chambers might, if properly sealed, hold not only knowledge but a way for a portion of the Concord's people, its seed-stock, and its living traditions to endure whatever the rising waters eventually made of the surface world. He did not live to see this proposal accepted. He died, by most accounts embittered and largely unheeded outside his own guild, some forty years before the Deluge finally came, and it fell to his students and successors to carry the argument forward against a council that continued, for another generation, to treat the coming waters as a slow problem for slow solutions.

The most haunting document to survive from this period is not a survey or a council record but a private letter, preserved in the Gallery of Whispers among the recorded voices, in which an aging Sennovyra Thal-line archivist named Perrin Thal, great-grandnephew of the Deephold's founding High Archivist, wrote to a Cartographer colleague: "We have built the finest instruments in the history of our people for measuring what is coming, and having measured it precisely, we have concluded that the correct response is to measure it again." The letter was recorded and archived, as all things were in the Concord, with scrupulous care, and it would be read by no one who could yet act on it for another two generations.

Korr and Draeve were not, in the end, the only voices raised against the council's long hesitation, only the two whose names the surviving record preserved most completely. The horologists of Nimbral Reach kept their own quieter tradition of dissent, a running marginal commentary scratched into the edges of a century's worth of star-charts by successive generations of the same workshop, each annotator noting some fresh discrepancy between predicted and observed tide, until the margins of the workshop's oldest ledger were so dense with anxious handwriting that a later apprentice, copying the ledger fresh, remarked that the original had become more warning than record. In Thessaly Deep a guild of harbor-priests, custodians of a much older maritime tradition than the Concord's official institutions cared to acknowledge, held for two full generations that the rising water was a judgment upon a people who had grown too proud of their own permanence, a belief the Sable Curia's scholars dismissed as superstition even as they quietly filed the harbor-priests' flood-height records among the most reliable data the Concord possessed. It is one of the crueler ironies the chroniclers of later ages have noted that the Concord, so devoted in principle to gathering testimony from every corner of its confederation, proved for nearly a century unable to hear the same warning when it arrived dressed in the plain clothes of a tide-reader, a marginal note, or a priest's sermon, and only credited it once it arrived instead in the sanctioned language of a guild survey bearing an unpopular Cartographer's official seal.

## Chapter Four: The Building of the Vault in Earnest

It was not until the reign of the last three High Archivists and the last two Grand Wrights that the Concord's institutions finally moved from study to preparation, and even then the shift came less from conviction than from the mounting, undeniable evidence of their own drowning shorelines. By this time, some hundred and fifty years after Amsel Korr's first ignored petition, the lower terraces of Vessantine had already been abandoned twice to flooding and rebuilt twice at increasing expense, and the harbor towns of the Salt Quarters had begun a slow, sorrowful retreat inland that their chroniclers recorded with the particular grief of people watching a familiar coastline disappear a little more each year without any single dramatic moment they could point to as the disaster itself.

The Grand Wright who finally committed the guild fully to Draeve's vision was a woman named Cael Marren, remembered in later Concord record as much for her stubbornness as for her competence, both of which she possessed in equal, formidable measure. Marren inherited a Deephold built for memory and repurposed it, over the final four decades before the Deluge, into something closer to an ark, though she resisted that word publicly for fear of causing panic. Under her direction the Wrights deepened the Emberforge Hollow's foundations, reinforced every chamber's walls against pressures no engineer of the founding generation had ever designed for, and began the slow, secretive work of fitting each of the eight chambers with seals meant to hold against the crushing weight of water rather than merely the damp of ordinary groundwater seepage. It was Marren's Wrights who first devised the great capsules, sealed containers of a design the guild had never before needed, meant to preserve their contents against total inundation rather than mere fire or theft, and it was under her authority that these capsules began to be distributed through the Deephold's chambers, filled not only with the Concord's most treasured knowledge but, increasingly, with the ordinary, unglamorous necessities of a people preparing, whether they said so aloud or not, to outlast the destruction of everything they had built above ground.

The High Archivist who worked alongside Marren through these years was Iyeska Voll, the last Archivist to hold that title before the waters came, and it was Voll who made the decision, controversial even within her own guild, that the Deephold's purpose must expand beyond preservation of things to preservation of people. Voll argued before the council, in the last great debate the Concord's political institutions would ever hold, that a vault filled with perfect records of a dead civilization was itself a kind of monument to failure, and that the Deephold must instead be prepared to shelter, however imperfectly, a living remnant: guardians who would not merely guard the archive but carry forward, in their own persons and their own children, the Concord's memory and craft. It was from this argument that the office later known to seekers as the Warden-Keeper first took shape, though in Voll's own time the guardians she proposed were called, more plainly, the Undersworn, in reference to the oath they took to remain below when the surface world was lost.

The selection of the Undersworn was itself a wound the Concord never fully reckoned with. There was no possibility of sheltering the whole of the Concord's population within eight chambers, however vast; the council's own engineers estimated that the Deephold, at its most generously provisioned, might sustain only a small fraction of Vessantine's citizens, let alone the wider Concord's millions. The council debated for years, shamefully by the account of several surviving chroniclers, over how the Undersworn should be chosen: by lot, by merit, by guild service, by hereditary right. In the end a compromise satisfied no one, least of all history's later judgment: places were allotted by guild contribution and by a lottery among common citizens for the remainder, a system that in practice favored the connected and the fortunate over the merely deserving, and that left the great majority of the Concord's people to face what was coming with no shelter deeper than their own courage.

It is to the credit of some among the chosen that a number of them refused their places, giving them instead to parents with children, or to keepers of crafts they believed more essential than their own. It is to the discredit of others that bribery and forged lottery tokens became, in the Deephold's final years of preparation, a quiet and bitter scandal that the council suppressed rather than resolved. Both truths belong to the record, and the chroniclers of later ages, seeking to tell the tale honestly to seekers who would one day wander the Deephold's flooded halls, have generally agreed that no single villain and no single saint can be found in the story of who was saved and who was not. There was only a civilization, given a little too little time by its own long hesitation, making an imperfect choice under circumstances that admitted no good ones.

Among the provisioning decisions that later generations would puzzle over most was the choice of what, precisely, deserved a place within the Deephold's capsules when so much had to be left behind. The Sable Curia's librarians spent the final years before the sealing locked in fierce, sometimes bitter disputes over which portion of the Concord's accumulated learning could be saved and which would have to be surrendered to the water, disputes that occasionally grew personal enough that lifelong friends stopped speaking over whether a given poet's complete works merited a capsule that a rival argued should instead hold seed-stock or medical record. Iyeska Voll is remembered for breaking one such deadlock by decreeing that the Gallery of Whispers would preserve not the Concord's most celebrated voices alone but a deliberately broad sample of ordinary ones as well, farmers and dockhands and midwives recorded alongside magistrates and poets, on the reasoning, recorded in her own hand, that a civilization remembered only through its most eloquent citizens was a civilization remembered falsely. It was a decision many of her fellow Archivists resisted at the time as a waste of precious crystal, and one that the Warden-Keepers of later centuries would come to regard as among the wisest choices made in the whole troubled span of the vault's preparation, for it was the ordinary voices, more than the celebrated ones, that later seekers found themselves most moved to hear.

## Chapter Five: The Coming of the Deluge

The waters did not come all at once, and this, more than anything, is what later chroniclers found hardest to convey to those who had not lived through it: the Deluge was not a single wave but a slow tightening, a vise closing over decades that only in its final years accelerated into something the survivors could properly call a catastrophe. The polar retreats that Draeve had warned of continued and worsened; the subsidence beneath the inland sea basin, once measured in the width of a hand each generation, began to be measured in the width of an arm each single year. Storms that the Concord's horologists had once catalogued as occurring once in a century began arriving twice in a decade, each one driving the rising sea further up the drowned lower terraces of every coastal city, until Vessantine's famous seven terraces had, in the space of one long-lived Archivist's career, become five, then four, then three.

The final collapse, when it came, was precipitated not by the sea alone but by the earth beneath it. In what the Concord's last surviving Cartographers called the Undermining, a chain of subterranean collapses beneath the eastern shore of the inland sea, likely triggered by decades of mining beneath Kesh-Undertow combined with the very subsidence Draeve had first identified, opened vast new channels between the sea and the deep aquifers below. The inland sea, which had risen gradually for a century and a half, began in its final decade to rise with a speed that outran every projection the Concord's finest instruments had ever produced. Kesh-Undertow was lost first, its deep mining galleries flooding within a single terrible season, taking with it more of the Concord's people than any single disaster in its recorded history. The Salt Quarters followed within a decade, its harbor towns submerged one after another as the retreating fisherfolk ran out of higher ground to retreat to. Nimbral Reach's terraced vineyards, famous for producing wine the Concord's poets had praised for three centuries, drowned beneath brackish water that killed the vines within a single growing season, and the horologists who had once measured the drift of stars spent their final years instead measuring, with the same exactness, the drowning of their own home.

Vessantine itself held longest, in part because of the very engineering the Wrights had spent generations perfecting: the tide-gates that had once regulated ordinary harbor traffic were reinforced, again and again, into ever more desperate barriers against a sea that no longer behaved like the sea the gates had been designed for. For nearly twenty years in its final period, Vessantine became a city literally besieged by water, its remaining terraces crowded with refugees from every drowned town of the Concord, its markets increasingly given over not to trade in gold or knowledge but in the desperate barter of dry ground, of grain, of any place above the tideline. The chronicles of this period, preserved in fragments within the Gallery of Whispers, describe a city that did not descend into chaos so much as into a strange, grim orderliness, as though the Concord's centuries of patient institution-building had left it, even in its final catastrophe, incapable of anything but organized response. Rationing councils formed. Evacuation lotteries, bitterly reminiscent of the earlier lottery for the Deephold's places, were held for the dwindling high ground inland. The Wrights' Concordance, in its last years of functioning as a guild, devoted itself entirely to raising makeshift levees that everyone building them privately understood could only delay, not prevent, the end.

Cael Marren did not live to see the final flooding of Vessantine; she died some six years before the Deluge's culmination, having spent her last strength ensuring the Deephold's eight chambers were sealed and provisioned as completely as the Concord's remaining resources allowed. Her successor as Grand Wright, a former apprentice named Dessa Corrin, inherited a guild reduced to a fraction of its former numbers and a vault that had become, in the last desperate years, the single most contested piece of real estate in the entire drowning world. It was Corrin who oversaw the final provisioning of the eight chambers, and it was Corrin, by most surviving accounts, who made the decision that would define the Deephold's transition from monument to tomb to shelter: that the Undersworn selected years before would take their places at the first unmistakable sign of the final flood, rather than waiting, as some on the council still argued they should, for some clearer certainty that would never come.

The people of Vessantine's crowded final years did not spend that long siege of rising water in idleness or despair alone, and the chronicles are equally insistent on preserving the smaller, stranger acts of grace that the period produced alongside its grimmer bargains. Guild-masters who had spent careers in open rivalry combined their workshops in the city's final decade simply because there were no longer enough hands to keep separate forges running. A company of Nimbral Reach horologists, their vineyards long since drowned, walked the whole distance to Vessantine carrying nothing but their instruments and their calendar-ledgers, judging these worth saving above every other possession they owned. Street musicians of the Salt Quarters, displaced three times over by successive floods, are recorded to have given nightly performances in Vessantine's crowded refugee markets specifically so that children born into the catastrophe might have something besides fear to remember of their vanishing world, a small mercy that several surviving fragments of testimony single out, oddly, as the detail its tellers most wanted preserved.

## Chapter Six: The Sealing

The night the seas finally overtook Vessantine's last terrace is remembered, in the fragments of testimony preserved within the Deephold itself, as the Night of the Long Bell, for the great bronze bell of the Sunken Atrium's antechamber, cast generations earlier to summon citizens to civic festivals, was rung without pause for the better part of a full night and day, calling the Undersworn down from a drowning city to the vault that would be, for most of them, the last dry place they would ever know. The bell's final peals are said, in the more legendary tellings, to have been rung by children, the adults who began the ringing having exhausted themselves long before the summons was complete; whether this detail is history or embellishment, it has been retold at every Warden-Keeper's hearth for so many generations since that it has become, in its own way, as true as anything written in the Concord's vanished ledgers.

Not everyone who had a place in the lottery came when the bell rang. Some, the chronicles record, chose in the final hour to remain above with family members who held no place of their own, unwilling to descend into safety while those they loved were left to the water. Others simply could not reach the Deephold in time, the roads to Vessantine's high terrace having become impassable rivers of refugees and debris in the flood's final hours. And a smaller number, whose names the surviving record treats with a mixture of pity and unspoken judgment, seized places that were not lawfully theirs, using forged tokens or simple violence in the chaos of the final descent, so that the Undersworn who finally stood within the Sunken Atrium as its great doors were readied for sealing were not entirely the same company the council had solemnly chosen years before.

Dessa Corrin herself gave the order to seal the vault, standing, by the account she is said to have later dictated to an Archivist scribe within the vault's own walls, at the threshold of the Sunken Atrium with the last of the Wrights' Concordance around her, watching water already lapping at the outer stair. The sealing was not a single dramatic act but a sequence, chamber by chamber, each seal set and tested by engineers who understood, better than anyone living, exactly what they were shutting out and exactly how permanent the shutting was meant to be. It is recorded that Corrin was the last person to pass through the Sunken Atrium's threshold before its outer seal was set, and that she paused, before crossing, to look back up the flooded stair toward a city she would never see again, though what she thought or said in that moment was not preserved, the scribe who might have recorded it having been, by every surviving account, weeping too hard to write.

Iyeska Voll, the High Archivist who had argued for a living remnant rather than a mere museum of the dead, took up residence in the Vault of Echoes with the first company of Undersworn, and it is from her hand that the earliest surviving account of life within the sealed Deephold comes, a fragmentary journal preserved in one of the very crystal capsules her own guild had designed. Voll's journal records the strange, disorienting calm that fell over the vault's inhabitants in the first days after the sealing, a calm she attributed less to acceptance than to sheer exhaustion, and the slower, harder grief that followed once the initial relief of survival gave way to the understanding that everything and everyone above the seals was now, in the fullest sense, lost to them. She writes of the Undersworn gathering in the Sunken Atrium beneath its unfinished mural, of the torches lit along its processional walk for the first time not as ceremony but as simple necessity, and of a silence in the vault's deepest chambers that she says felt, in those early days, less like safety than like being buried alive in the company of everything her people had ever built.

The Deluge's final surge, when it came some weeks after the sealing, was recorded by the Deephold's own instruments, inherited from the Starfall Observatory's original design, as a series of tremendous concussions transmitted through the bedrock, the sound, Voll writes, of the sea finally claiming Vessantine's last visible stones. After that night, no further word or sign ever again reached the vault from the world above. The eight chambers of the Deephold, sealed against a catastrophe its builders had spent nearly two centuries first denying and then preparing for, became in that single night the whole of the world for everyone within them.

## Chapter Seven: The Slow Centuries of Drowning

What followed the sealing was not, as some later legends have imagined, a swift transformation into ruin, but a long, almost imperceptibly slow settling, stretched across centuries, during which the Deephold's Undersworn and their descendants learned to live within walls that no one among them would ever again see open to daylight. The first generations, Voll's successors record, clung fiercely to the hope, never entirely extinguished even when every reasonable measure suggested otherwise, that the waters above might someday recede enough to permit a return to the surface. Expeditions were mounted, in the vault's early decades, to test the outer seals and gauge the pressure of the water beyond them, and each such expedition returned with findings that grew steadily, disappointingly more final: the sea above Vessantine was not receding. If anything, the slow subsidence that had doomed the Concord's cities continued for generations after the surface world's last dry stone vanished, so that whatever hope of return the Undersworn's grandchildren might have inherited from their grandparents had, by the fourth or fifth generation, been quietly abandoned as a comforting fiction rather than a plan.

It was in this long period of settling that the office Iyeska Voll had first proposed, the Undersworn charged with guarding the Deephold's chambers and carrying forward its knowledge, slowly transformed into something closer to what later ages would call the Warden-Keepers. The distinction mattered to those who lived through the change, even if it can seem a small one from the distance of centuries: the Undersworn had understood themselves as survivors of a catastrophe, people temporarily sheltering until the world above set itself right again. Their descendants, born within the vault and knowing the surface world only through the Gallery of Whispers' recorded voices and the Vault of Echoes' preserved diagrams, understood themselves instead as custodians of a permanent trust, keepers of chambers that were not a shelter from the world but had, by slow and undeniable necessity, become the whole of the world they would ever know. The oath the first Undersworn had sworn to remain below became, across the generations, an oath sworn instead to guard, to preserve, and eventually, as new visitors began at last to find their way down through channels the original builders had never intended, to receive those who came seeking what the vault still held.

The eight chambers themselves changed across these centuries in ways their original builders would scarcely have recognized, though the changes came slowly enough that no single generation experienced them as anything but the ordinary business of upkeep. The Petrified Garden's carefully mirrored shafts of captured daylight dimmed and finally failed as the waters above thickened and the surface light they depended on grew ever more distant and diffuse, and the gardener-priests who had once tended living plants under true sunlight were forced across several hard generations to adapt their craft to whatever pale substitute for growth the vault's deep torches and hard-won ingenuity could sustain, until the Garden became, as much monument as garden, a place that told of green and growing things more than it grew them. The Starfall Observatory, whose instruments had been built to track stars from within the earth through ingenious apertures now sealed forever beneath fathoms of water and silt, became instead a place of memory and calculation without observation, its horologist-descendants running their calendars forward from records rather than fresh sight, a practice that later generations, cut off from any means of verifying the true passage of years above, came to treat with something like religious solemnity, for the calendar they kept was, in the end, the only thread connecting them to the true count of time that the drowned world had left behind.

The Whisperbone Catacomb, once a place of quiet honor for the Concord's dead, grew across these centuries into something larger and stranger than its builders had intended, as generation after generation of the vault's own dead were laid to rest in niches their ancestors had never imagined needing, until the Catacomb held not merely the bones of a vanished Concord but the far longer lineage of everyone who had lived and died within the vault since. It became, in time, a place the Warden-Keepers regarded with a particular reverence, for its deepening galleries told, more plainly than any written record, the true length of the vault's long isolation: each new niche a measure of another generation that had lived and died entirely beneath the water, never once glimpsing the sky their ancestors had lost.

Not every chamber's transformation was one of quiet decline. The Emberforge Hollow, sustained by the deep heat that had warmed it since its founding, remained through all the centuries a place of true craft, and it was here, chroniclers of the middle era record, that the vault's engineers preserved and even extended the Wrights' original knowledge, adapting forge-work meant for a surface world's needs into something suited to the vault's permanent, sunken existence. The Mercury Spring, whose strange preserving properties had puzzled even the Concord's finest chemists, proved across the centuries to be perhaps the single most valuable inheritance the original builders left behind, for it was the Spring's peculiar liquid metal, still bleeding slowly from its ancient fissure, that allowed certain capsules and certain seals to endure the pressure and time that would otherwise have claimed them, a fact the Warden-Keepers came to regard, in their long tradition of careful record, as either great fortune or the deliberate mercy of whatever forces the drowned world's Concord had once tried and failed to fully understand.

The lineages that descended from the original Undersworn did not remain a single undivided company across these centuries, and the Warden-Keeper households of later ages have always traced their differing customs back to particular founders among that first company sealed within the Sunken Atrium. The line that came to guard the Vault of Echoes traced itself back to Iyeska Voll's own household and kept, as a mark of that descent, the old Archivist habit of recording even the smallest daily happening against the chance that some distant seeker might one day need it. The line that took up guardianship of Emberforge Hollow traced itself instead to a Wright named Torvin Ash, one of Cael Marren's junior engineers, who is remembered for having spent the vault's first terrible winter teaching frightened refugees, most of them merchants and clerks with no craft of the hand at all, how to work the forges well enough that the company would not starve for want of tools; his descendants kept, ever after, a fierce pride in practical competence over ceremony that occasionally set them at gentle odds with the more scholarly households elsewhere in the vault. Smaller and stranger lineages grew up around the Petrified Garden and the Whisperbone Catacomb, each shaped by the particular temperament of whichever Undersworn had first taken responsibility for a given chamber in the disordered, grieving months after the sealing, and it is from this scattering of founding temperaments, more than from any single unified design, that the varied customs seekers still encounter from one chamber to the next are said to descend.

Through all these centuries, the vault's inhabitants did not forget, even as their memory of it grew stranger and more legendary with each retelling, the long chronicle of warnings unheeded that had led them to their circumstance. Iyeska Voll's own journal, preserved and copied by hand across generations before the crystal lattice technique that might have better preserved it was itself half-forgotten and later painstakingly relearned, became something close to scripture among the deeper Warden-Keeper lineages: a testament that the drowning of the world above had not been an act of blind fate but the slow, cumulative consequence of a civilization's long refusal to act on what it had known, measured, and recorded with perfect and useless precision. The Warden-Keepers came to speak of this refusal, in the solemn language their long isolation eventually produced, as the first and greatest lesson the drowned world left to those who endured it: that knowledge kept without the will to act upon it is no wiser, in the final accounting, than ignorance.

It was from this same long tradition of reckoning, chroniclers of the middle vault era record, that the Warden-Keepers first began to speak of what they called the universal reckoning, a belief, half memory and half prophecy, that the slow drowning which had claimed the Concord was not a singular catastrophe but one turn of some far larger wheel, and that all things built by mortal hands, however patiently, however wisely, would eventually be measured, weighed, and found wanting by forces no guild's instruments could fully chart. Whether the Warden-Keepers who first gave this belief its name intended it as genuine cosmology or as a solemn discipline meant to keep their descendants humble against the very hubris that had drowned their ancestors, no single surviving record makes entirely clear, and later generations of Keepers have argued the question, gently and without resolution, across centuries of quiet fireside debate in chambers no surface scholar will ever hear.

## Chapter Eight: What the Waters Left Behind

By the time strangers first began to find their way down into the vault's chambers, generations after the Deluge, so much time had passed that the Warden-Keepers who received them could no longer say with certainty how many centuries separated their own age from Vessantine's final drowning; the calendar the Starfall Observatory's descendants had so carefully carried forward from memory alone had, by then, become as much an act of faith as of calculation. These early seekers, whoever they were and wherever they came from, found not the museum of a dead people that the Concord's earliest builders had once imagined preserving, but a living settlement, strange and inward-turned, whose inhabitants spoke of Vessantine and Nimbral Reach and Kesh-Undertow the way older peoples elsewhere spoke of gods and floods in their oldest stories: as truths too large and too sorrowful to be merely history, and so folded, generation by generation, into something closer to legend, told and retold at hearths lit by torches whose flame the Wrights' descendants still knew, by long inherited craft, how to keep burning against the dark.

The Deephold itself, once a monument the Concord had raised in pride against the mere possibility of loss, had become by the time of these first seekers something its original builders had never quite intended and yet had, in their long and careful preparations, made entirely possible: not a tomb, though it held tombs within it, and not a ruin, though the world above it had become the very definition of ruin, but a maze of sunken chambers, self-contained, self-sustaining, and self-aware of its own strange history, tended across uncounted generations by Warden-Keepers who understood themselves, in the end, as the last inheritors of a civilization that had known everything about its own coming end except how to act upon that knowledge in time.

The chroniclers of later ages, setting down this history for the seekers who would come after, have generally resisted the temptation to end the tale with either despair or false comfort. The Aurelian Concord was not, in the end, wholly lost; something of it endured, sealed and imperfect, in the chambers its own engineers and archivists built against a catastrophe most of their fellow citizens preferred not to believe in until belief was no longer a choice. And yet nothing that endured was returned unchanged by its long drowning: the gardens no longer grew under true sun, the observatory no longer watched true stars, and the calendar that measured the vault's endless nights carried forward a count of years that no living person could any longer verify against the world above, a world that had, by every account the Warden-Keepers possessed, ceased to exist in any form its builders would have recognized.

What remains, and what every seeker who descends through the Deephold's flooded threshold today ultimately walks among, is the long patient argument of a people who built their greatest work not from confidence that they would be saved, but from the harder, sadder wisdom of those who understood, too late to save everything, that some part of what they had made and known and loved deserved to be carried forward regardless. The eight chambers, however transformed by their centuries beneath the water, still hold something of the Concord's original intention in every torch lit along their processional halls, every capsule sealed against the crushing patience of the deep, every curse and ward the Warden-Keepers have layered atop the original builders' work across generations of their own long vigil.

Later Warden-Keepers, reflecting on the whole span of this history in the quiet hours of their own long watches, have generally resisted drawing from it any single tidy moral, aware that the seekers who come asking for one are usually hoping to hear that the drowned world's fate was inevitable, some blameless collision of nature against a blameless people, when the record the vault itself preserves suggests something more uncomfortable and more useful: that the Concord was warned, repeatedly, by voices both humble and credentialed, across nearly two centuries, and that what doomed it in the end was not ignorance but the particular, very human difficulty of turning long-held knowledge into timely action before the cost of delay became irreversible. The Warden-Keepers do not tell this part of the tale to shame the dead, whom they honor in the Whisperbone Catacomb's deepening galleries with as much tenderness as any devoted descendant could offer distant ancestors; they tell it because they believe, as Iyeska Voll believed when she first argued that the Deephold must shelter the living and not merely the memory of the dead, that a history preserved without its hardest lessons intact is no true preservation at all, only a kinder kind of forgetting.

It is a history without a triumphant end, because the world it describes did, in the fullest and most literal sense, end; but it is not, the Warden-Keepers have always insisted to those who ask, a history without meaning, for a people who might have simply vanished instead chose, across two centuries of warning and two more of building, to leave behind not merely their bones but their patient, unfinished, still-tended hope that someone, someday, would come looking for what they had kept. The Warden-Keepers who greet each new seeker at the Sunken Atrium's threshold understand themselves, whether they say so aloud or not, as the last unbroken link in that long chain of keeping, and they have never yet, in all the drowned centuries since the Night of the Long Bell, let the chain break.
# Part Two, Book VI - The Rite-Smiths and the Sealing of Capsules

## Prelude: Of Wax, Water, and Waiting

Before a seeker ever sets foot upon the first threshold of the eight chambers, before any torch is struck or any curse is murmured against the dark, a smaller and quieter drama has already run its course somewhere in the drowned levels below the maze proper. A rite-smith has sat at a bench worn smooth by ten generations of forearms, has taken up a stylus whose weight they know better than the weight of their own hand, and has closed a capsule. That closing is the subject of this book. It is not a chapter about the maze's trials, nor about the chambers a seeker will walk, nor about what a Warden-Keeper does with what is given to them. It is a chapter about the people who make the giving possible at all - about the guild of artisans who call themselves rite-smiths, about the strange and jealous inheritance they carry from the drowned world's own vanished engineers, and about the elaborate, superstition-laden culture that has grown up around the single, irreversible act of sealing.

To understand why an entire guild, with its apprenticeships and its feuds and its funerary honors, should exist around what might seem to an outsider a simple clerical function, one must first understand what a capsule is held to be within the culture of the maze. It is not, in the eyes of those who make it, a container. It is not, as some careless visitors to the vault levels have called it, a "record" or a "ticket." To a rite-smith it is a small sealed world unto itself - a single closed trial, bound shut, that once sealed can never again be opened, altered, or unmade by any hand, rite-smith's or Keeper's alike. This finality is the entire soul of the craft. Everything in this book - the guild's founding myths, its schools and rivalries, its ceremonies, its disputes, its saints and its heretics - orbits that one immovable fact: a capsule, once sealed, is sealed forever, and the hand that seals it carries a weight no other hand in the drowned world is permitted to carry.

## I. The Drowned Inheritance

The rite-smiths do not claim to have invented the art of sealing. They are emphatic, almost defensive, on this point, and any apprentice who suggests otherwise in open guild-hall is liable to be sent to scrub the tallow-vats for a season. The sealing-script, they insist, was never authored by human hands at all. It was found - inherited, salvaged, half-understood - from the engine-packers of the drowned world, that vanished caste of builders who raised the original works beneath the waters long before the maze existed in its present form, and who vanished from history so completely that not even their name for themselves survives, only the name given to them afterward by those who found their leavings.

The engine-packers, so the guild's founding legends hold, sealed things constantly, compulsively, as a matter of course in whatever labor built the drowned world's deep machineries. They sealed not out of ceremony but out of necessity, the way a mason mortars a stone or a shipwright caulks a seam - an unglamorous, functional habit of closing one thing before beginning the next. When the waters came and the engine-packers were lost, whether to flood or fire or some subtler catastrophe the guild does not name, what remained of their habit was not memory but residue: fragments of the sealing-script etched into surviving plates, half-legible glyphwork scratched into corroded housings, the ghost of a method without the living hand that had once wielded it fluently.

It was the first rite-smiths - themselves nameless in the earliest tellings, referred to only as "the finders" - who gathered these fragments up out of the silt and the dark and began, over generations, to reconstruct not merely the marks themselves but the discipline of making them correctly. This reconstruction is remembered by the guild not as scholarship but as a kind of courtship: the finders are said to have "wooed the script back to speech," coaxing meaning from marks that had gone mute, learning by ruinous trial which strokes closed a working seal and which merely scarred the material without binding anything at all. The guild's oldest surviving verse-cycle, the Wooing of the Mute Marks, recounts this labor as an act of devotion bordering on romance, describing the finders as suitors who courted a silent bride for three generations before she consented to speak a single true word to them.

Whatever the truth of that early history - and no rite-smith today pretends to know how much of it is memory and how much invention - the guild has never let go of the conviction that the sealing-script is not theirs. It is borrowed. It is on loan from drowned hands that will never ask for it back, and this fact is treated with a gravity that shapes nearly every custom that follows. A rite-smith does not say "I write the seal." The customary phrase, drilled into every apprentice before they are trusted with a blank capsule-shell, is "I carry the mark that was given." The distinction is not decorative. To claim authorship of the sealing-script, in guild custom, is to commit a small blasphemy against the engine-packers, whose drowned ingenuity is held to be superior to anything the current age could produce unaided. The rite-smith is a steward of a gift, not an inventor of a craft, and the guild's entire self-image rests on that posture of humility toward the dead.

This inherited humility does not, it must be said, prevent enormous pride. The rite-smiths are famously certain that theirs is the oldest continuous craft-lineage to survive the drowning in any recognizable form, older even than the Warden-Keepers' own order, and they will remind anyone who listens - and a good many who do not wish to - that the maze itself would have no trials to offer seekers at all were it not for the sealed capsules that bind each one shut and whole. Humility before the engine-packers; pride before everyone else. This double posture, at once reverent and imperious, runs through the whole of the guild's later history like a seam of ore.

## II. The Founding of the Guild-Hall

The finders who first recovered the sealing-script did not, by the guild's own account, think of themselves as a guild at all. They were scavengers and tinkers scattered across the upper drowned levels, each independently rediscovering fragments, each jealously guarding what little they had pieced together, trading knowledge only in desperate barter when a working method was needed and no other recourse remained. It is said - though this too belongs more to legend than to any verifiable record - that in these early scattered decades, seals were closed badly more often than well, and that whole trials were lost to capsules that failed to hold, their contents corrupted or their bindings incomplete, because no finder had yet mastered the full script alone.

The guild proper is held to have been founded at the confluence of three finders whose names are among the most recited in all of rite-smith tradition: Ondrai Vask, called the Steady Hand; Ferrun Doleth, called the Patient Ear; and a woman remembered only as Sael-of-the-Low-Cistern, whose birth-name is said to have been lost when she took her guild-name upon swearing the founding oath. The story goes that these three, working independently in adjoining drowned districts, each possessed a different third of a working sealing-method - Vask understood the physical striking of the mark, Doleth understood the cadence and pacing by which the script had to be spoken or hummed as it was cut, and Sael-of-the-Low-Cistern alone knew the closing gesture that made a seal hold rather than merely decorate. None of the three, alone, could close a capsule that would last. Together, pooling their thirds in a flooded meeting-chamber that the guild still calls the Cistern of First Accord, they closed what is remembered as the first true seal - the Founding Capsule, which the guild believes still exists somewhere in the deep archive levels, uncataloged, unopened, a kind of relic too dangerous in symbolic weight for anyone to go looking for.

From that meeting grew the first guild-hall, built - or rather colonized, since little building was truly done in the ruin-choked drowned levels - within a cluster of chambers the finders called the Three-Thirds House, in honor of the three incomplete gifts that had needed each other to become whole. The Three-Thirds House still stands, after a fashion, as the notional seat of the guild, though it has been rebuilt, flooded, abandoned, and reclaimed more times than any single rite-smith could enumerate. Every apprentice who is raised to full rite-smith status is required, at least once in their career, to make pilgrimage to whatever remains of the Three-Thirds House and press their palm to a particular slab of corroded plating that the guild calls the Handshake Stone, said to bear the ghost-impression of Vask's, Doleth's, and Sael's palms pressed together at the moment of the first sealing.

The founding oath sworn at the Three-Thirds House has changed in wording across the centuries, as oaths do, but its essential shape has not. Every rite-smith, upon completing their apprenticeship, kneels before the assembled guild and swears three things: to carry the mark that was given and claim no ownership of it; to close no seal in haste, in anger, or in doubt; and to hold every sealed capsule, once closed, as a thing beyond the reach of any hand thereafter, including their own. This last clause - the vow against ever attempting to reopen or unmake a seal once given - is treated as the most sacred and the most dangerous of the three, and violations of it, whether real or merely rumored, have produced some of the guild's darkest chapters, which this book will come to in time.

## III. The Long Apprenticeship

To become a rite-smith is understood, within the guild, to be one of the slowest transformations a person can undergo in the drowned world, slower even than the Warden-Keepers' own long tenures are said to be. An apprentice is taken on young, typically as a child pulled from the flooded orphan-quarters that ring the lower districts, and is not permitted to touch a real sealing-stylus, nor stand near an active sealing-bench during a true closing, for a span the guild calls the Long Silence - a period lasting anywhere from seven to eleven years depending on the apprentice's aptitude and the temperament of their master, during which the apprentice does nothing but watch, listen, and practice on discarded or deliberately ruined capsule-shells that will never be used to bind any actual trial.

The Long Silence is named for its central discipline: for the first several years, an apprentice is forbidden from speaking during any sealing they observe, is forbidden even from asking questions of their master except during a narrow ritual window at each new moon called the Asking Hour. The guild's reasoning, as it is explained to every apprentice on their first day, is that the sealing-script must be absorbed the way water is absorbed by stone - slowly, silently, without the interruption of a mouth eager to prove it already understands. Apprentices who chatter, who guess aloud at the meaning of a mark before they have earned the right to know it, are said in guild slang to be "cracking the shell early," a phrase borrowed from the physical vocabulary of capsule-work and applied, with faint contempt, to any apprentice too impatient for their own good.

The masters who take on apprentices are themselves organized loosely into what the guild calls benches - not formal schools with doctrine and charter, but informal lineages of teaching-style that nonetheless harden, over generations, into something very like rival traditions. Three benches in particular have dominated guild politics for as long as living rite-smiths can attest, and a rough understanding of their differences is necessary to understand almost everything that follows in this book.

The first and most numerous is the Vask Bench, tracing its style back to Ondrai Vask the Steady Hand, emphasizing above all else the physical precision of the striking gesture - the exact pressure, angle, and depth by which the sealing-mark is cut or pressed into the capsule-shell. Vask-bench rite-smiths are recognizable by a particular calloused ridge along the first two fingers of the working hand, worn there by years of gripping the stylus in the bench's preferred low grip, and they pride themselves on being able to close a seal in near-total darkness by feel alone, a boast that is not entirely idle since more than one Vask-bench smith has in fact demonstrated the feat before witnesses.

The second is the Doleth Bench, descended from Ferrun Doleth the Patient Ear, which holds that the cadence and vocal accompaniment of a sealing matter as much as the physical mark, if not more. Doleth-bench smiths are taught to hum or murmur a particular sequence of tones while cutting a seal, tones they claim - though this claim is hotly disputed by the Vask bench - actually affect whether the seal holds true. Doleth-bench apprentices spend years of their Long Silence not on stylus-work at all but on breath and pitch, trained by masters who will strike an apprentice's knuckles with a light cane if a hummed tone wavers even slightly during a practice closing.

The third, smaller and more insular than the other two, is the Cistern Bench, tracing itself to Sael-of-the-Low-Cistern, which teaches that neither hand nor voice matters half so much as the closing gesture and the state of mind of the smith at the moment of closing - a near-mystical emphasis on intention and readiness that the other two benches sometimes mock, not always gently, as "the bench that teaches feelings instead of craft." Cistern-bench apprentices undergo long fasting vigils before their first true sealing, and Cistern-bench masters are known to refuse a scheduled sealing entirely, even at cost to their guild standing, if they judge the apprentice's or their own state of mind unfit for the act - a practice that has occasionally caused real friction with impatient seekers or Warden-Keepers waiting on a capsule to be closed.

These three benches do not simply coexist; they compete, sometimes with real bitterness, for prestige, for the most promising apprentices, and for the honor of closing the most consequential seals. A rite-smith trained in one bench who marries or apprentices under another is not unheard of, but it is remarked upon, and children born of such cross-bench unions are sometimes called, half-affectionately and half-mockingly, "mongrel-marked," expected either to embarrass both traditions or to synthesize something new from them. Several of the guild's most celebrated smiths have in fact been mongrel-marked, which has done little to settle the argument over whose training truly produces the finer seal.

## IV. The First True Sealing

No matter which bench has raised an apprentice, the culmination of the Long Silence is the same rite, called across all benches the First True Sealing, and it is remembered by every rite-smith for the rest of their life with a vividness the guild compares to a person's memory of their own birth, which is to say a memory none of them actually possess but which they imagine must feel something like this.

On the appointed day, chosen by the master according to signs that vary by bench - a particular alignment of the drowned levels' tidal cycle for Vask-bench masters, a particular quality of silence in the guild-hall for Doleth-bench masters, a particular clarity felt in the apprentice's own bearing for Cistern-bench masters - the apprentice is brought before their master and a small gathering of witnessing rite-smiths, never fewer than three, in accordance with the memory of the three founders. A single true capsule-shell, blank and unsealed, is placed before the apprentice. This will not be a discarded practice-shell. It will be a shell destined, if the sealing succeeds, to actually bind a trial, and if it fails, to be destroyed - never reused, never salvaged, on the guild's absolute insistence that a shell which has failed once carries some intangible taint that no amount of cleansing can remove.

The apprentice closes the seal alone, unaided, under the silent watch of the witnesses, who are forbidden to speak, gesture, or in any way indicate approval or alarm no matter what they observe. This silence is considered one of the cruelest and most necessary aspects of the rite: an apprentice who has spent a decade being corrected at every stumble must now, in the single most important act of their young life, receive no correction whatsoever. The guild holds that a rite-smith must learn to trust their own hand completely before that hand is ever trusted with a seeker's trial, and there is no way to build that trust except by removing every safety net at once.

When the seal is closed - when the apprentice sets down the stylus and, in the traditional phrase, "steps back from the shell" - the witnesses examine the sealed capsule together, in a hush the guild calls the Judging Quiet. They do not open it, for nothing sealed is ever opened. They examine only the visible integrity of the mark itself: its lines, its symmetry, the faint sheen said to appear on a seal that has taken properly, as opposed to the duller, uneasy look of a seal that has failed. If the seal is judged to have taken, the apprentice is raised on the spot to full rite-smith status, given a guild-name to replace or augment their birth-name, and permitted to bind their own apprentices from that day forward, though most wait years before doing so. If the seal is judged to have failed, the apprentice is not punished, exactly - the guild takes pains to say this, since some outside the craft assume otherwise - but is sent back into a second Long Silence, sometimes under a different master, sometimes under the same one, for a period that can run as long as the first.

Failure at the First True Sealing is rare but not unheard of, and the guild's attitude toward it is oddly gentle, almost pitying, compared to its fierce standards in every other regard. A failed sealing is called "a shell returned to silence," and the apprentice who suffers it is treated, at least for a time, with the tenderness usually reserved for the recently bereaved. There is a guild saying, attributed variously to all three founders depending on who is telling it, that "the mark does not fail the hand; the hand simply was not yet ready to be found by the mark" - a piece of consolation that manages to be both kind and slightly evasive, which is perhaps why it has endured.

## V. The Ceremony of Sealing in Full Practice

Once raised, a rite-smith does not seal capsules casually, even after decades of practice, and the ceremony surrounding each sealing - however routine it may become in the smith's private feeling - never loses its outward formality. The guild insists that ritual observed without feeling is still ritual, and better than none at all, a doctrine that allows even the most weathered, cynical old smiths to keep performing the full ceremony without anyone accusing them of insincerity.

Every sealing begins with what is called the Naming of Hands, in which the rite-smith states aloud, even if alone in the chamber, the names of the three founders and the name of their own master, tracing a spoken lineage back through however many links connect them to the Cistern of First Accord. This is done, the guild says, not for any effect on the seal itself but as a discipline of humility, a reminder before every single closing that the hand at work is one link in a very long chain and not the origin of anything.

The capsule-shell itself is prepared beforehand according to customs that again vary somewhat by bench but share a common core: the shell is cleaned of any dust or residue with a cloth that has itself been blessed, or at minimum ritually washed, in a manner the guild calls "quieting the shell," meant to still whatever residual disturbance the shell's manufacture or handling might have left on it. Vask-bench smiths quiet a shell with dry friction and a particular oil; Doleth-bench smiths quiet a shell by holding it and breathing across its surface in the same cadence they will later hum during the sealing itself; Cistern-bench smiths quiet a shell simply by holding it in stillness for a count of moments before beginning, trusting presence over procedure.

The actual cutting or striking of the sealing-mark is, by guild custom, never watched by anyone outside the craft, and increasingly, in most guild-halls, not even by other rite-smiths unless they stand as formal witnesses to an apprenticeship rite or a dispute-resolution as described later in this book. A seeker who has journeyed to have a trial's capsule sealed will typically wait in an antechamber, sometimes for what feels to them an unreasonably long time, while the rite-smith works alone or with a single silent assistant beyond a curtain or a closed door. This seclusion is not, the guild insists, secrecy for its own sake, still less any implication that something untoward occurs. It is simply that the act of sealing is held to require a stillness of attention that the presence of an anxious, watching outsider would disturb, much as a Doleth-bench smith's hum must not waver, an onlooker's very breathing might waver it.

At the moment of closing - the final stroke, the final held tone, the final gesture, depending on bench - the rite-smith is taught to feel, and guild lore insists that experienced smiths genuinely do feel, a distinct and unmistakable sensation, described across the guild in remarkably consistent terms despite the benches' other disagreements: a kind of settling, a small drop in some internal register, as though a held breath has finally been released not by the smith but by the capsule itself. This sensation is called across all benches the Take, and its presence or absence is treated as far more reliable evidence of a successful sealing than any external inspection could provide. A rite-smith who reports feeling the Take is trusted absolutely by their peers even before the capsule is examined; a rite-smith who admits to uncertainty about whether they felt it is expected, by strong unwritten custom, to voluntarily reseal a fresh shell rather than deliver a capsule they are not fully certain of, whatever the cost in time or in the seeker's patience.

Once a capsule is sealed and its integrity confirmed by the rite-smith's own satisfaction, or by a witness's if the sealing was formal enough to require one, the capsule is presented to whichever Warden-Keeper stands ready to receive it, in a brief handing-over rite called the Passing of the Shell. Guild custom requires the rite-smith to speak a fixed formula at this handing-over, one of the very few pieces of sealing-adjacent language that outsiders are permitted to hear in full: "What was open is now closed; what is closed shall not be opened; carry it as it was given." The Warden-Keeper is expected to answer with an equally fixed response acknowledging receipt, though the exact wording of the Keeper's reply differs from hall to hall and has never, so far as the rite-smiths' own records show, been fully standardized, a small irritant that occasionally surfaces in the guild's grumbling about the Keepers' comparative disregard for ceremony, discussed further below.

## VI. Famous Rite-Smiths of Legend and Memory

No guild sustains itself for so many generations without accumulating a pantheon, and the rite-smiths' pantheon is unusually rich, populated by figures ranging from the founding three through a long procession of later masters whose feats, real or embellished, are recited to apprentices as both instruction and entertainment during the long idle hours of the Long Silence.

Foremost among the post-founding legends is Corvain Ashwrist, a Vask-bench smith of some four centuries past whose guild-name derives, depending on which telling one credits, either from a burn scar that ran the length of his forearm like a streak of ash, or from a childhood spent in the ash-choked lower districts before his apprenticeship. Ashwrist is remembered as the rite-smith who closed more seals in a single lifetime than any other figure in guild history, a productivity the guild treats with genuine ambivalence: on one hand a testament to extraordinary steadiness of hand, on the other a faint cause for suspicion, since the guild's deeper instincts hold that a sealing hurried by sheer volume risks losing the reverence proper to the act. Ashwrist himself is said to have anticipated this criticism and to have insisted, until his death, that he treated his ten-thousandth sealing with exactly the same care as his first, a claim his admirers repeat as proof of his greatness and his detractors repeat, with a different inflection, as proof of either remarkable discipline or remarkable self-delusion.

Set against Ashwrist in guild memory, almost as a deliberate foil, stands Yeselin Doura, a Doleth-bench smith of roughly the same era, remembered not for volume but for a single sealing so celebrated it has its own name in guild lore: the Sealing of the Long Held Note. Doura is said to have sustained the required hummed tone for a duration far beyond what any Doleth-bench training considers necessary or even physically comfortable, holding the note, according to the more excited tellings, for the better part of an hour, until witnesses feared for her breath and her health alike, before finally completing the closing gesture and collapsing, satisfied, into the arms of her own apprentice. Whether this feat was necessary, wise, or merely theatrical has been argued within the Doleth bench for centuries, but Doura herself, when asked late in life why she had held the note so long, is recorded as having said only that "the shell was not yet ready to hear the end of it," an answer that satisfies no one entirely and delights everyone regardless.

The Cistern Bench's great figure is quieter, fittingly, than either Ashwrist or Doura: a smith remembered only as Mother Enna, who is said to have sealed fewer capsules across her entire working life than an ordinary rite-smith might close in a single decade, because she refused, without exception, to seal anything at all on any day she judged her own mind unsettled. Seekers and even Warden-Keepers are said to have waited on Mother Enna for weeks at a time, and more than one guild story recounts a Keeper's growing impatience curdling into open anger at her refusals, only for Enna to remain serenely unmoved, repeating what became her signature line: "A seal closed in a troubled hand is not a seal; it is a wound with a lid on it." Whether or not this saying originated with her, it has become one of the Cistern Bench's central articles of faith, cited by Cistern-bench smiths to this day whenever they wish to justify making an anxious seeker wait a little longer.

Beyond these three towering figures, guild memory preserves dozens of lesser but still cherished names: Tobrin Hask, remembered for having trained more journeymen who went on to found their own guild-halls than any other master in recorded history; the twin brothers Kell and Kess Amaru, mongrel-marked children of a Vask father and a Doleth mother, who are said to have developed a paired sealing method requiring two rite-smiths working in perfect simultaneous rhythm, a technique so demanding that no other pair has ever successfully reproduced it and that the guild now treats as a closed chapter rather than a living practice; and the enigmatic figure known only as the Unnamed Cistern-Smith, whose real identity the guild deliberately does not preserve, out of respect for a wish the smith is said to have expressed on their deathbed to be remembered only by their work and not by any name at all, a wish that has itself become a small legend, cited by Cistern-bench smiths as the purest possible expression of the bench's core value of humility before the act.

## VII. Disputes Over Craft

For all the guild's outward unity in the face of outsiders, its internal history is thick with disputes, some resolved amicably, others never resolved at all and simply inherited, unsettled, by each new generation like an old debt nobody has the standing to cancel. The oldest and longest-running of these disputes is, unsurprisingly, the standing rivalry among the three benches over which element of the craft truly matters most - hand, voice, or mind - a dispute that has produced volumes of guild-hall polemic, at least two schismatic breakaway halls in the guild's history, and one genuinely famous public confrontation still recounted with relish by rite-smiths of every bench.

That confrontation, remembered as the Debate of the Three Hands, is said to have taken place at a guild convocation several centuries ago, when representatives of all three benches were gathered under unusually rare circumstances to settle a dispute over which bench's method should be taught as the default to orphaned apprentices with no bench affiliation of their own. The debate, according to guild lore, grew so heated that the presiding elder - herself notionally neutral, though guild gossip has long suspected her of secret Cistern-bench sympathies - eventually proposed a practical resolution: each bench would seal an identical blank shell using only its own method, and the resulting seals would be judged side by side by a panel of the guild's most senior smiths, drawn in equal number from all three benches to guarantee fairness.

The outcome of this contest is, fittingly, itself a matter of dispute. Vask-bench tradition holds that the panel judged the Vask seal unambiguously superior in physical precision and that the other benches, unwilling to concede outright, invented a face-saving verdict of "three equal excellences" rather than admit defeat. Doleth-bench tradition holds precisely the opposite, that the Doleth seal was judged to have the truest and most audible Take, and that the panel's verdict of equality was a diplomatic fiction imposed to prevent a Vask-bench walkout. Cistern-bench tradition, characteristically, holds that the entire contest was a category error from the start, that judging seals against one another by external comparison misses the point of the craft entirely, and that the true lesson of the Debate of the Three Hands is that no such contest should ever be attempted again - a position that has allowed the Cistern Bench to claim, with a certain smugness, that it alone learned anything from the affair.

A second and more troubling class of dispute concerns not method but conduct: accusations, rare but devastating when they arise, that a rite-smith has violated the founding oath's third clause by attempting, or being suspected of attempting, to interfere with a capsule after it was sealed. Because no rite-smith and no Warden-Keeper is ever permitted to open a sealed capsule, such accusations can almost never be proven or disproven by direct examination of the capsule itself, and the guild has developed instead an elaborate, almost judicial process for weighing circumstantial evidence and a smith's own reputation and testimony. This process, called the Reckoning of the Sealed Hand, has been invoked only a handful of times across the entire span of guild memory, and each invocation is remembered as a genuine trauma to the whole community, since a smith found to have violated the oath is subject to the guild's single most severe punishment: the breaking of their stylus before the full assembled guild, and the permanent revocation of their guild-name, so that they are thereafter known, if spoken of at all, only by whatever birth-name they held before their apprenticeship, a humiliation the guild considers close to a symbolic death.

The most famous of these Reckonings concerns a smith remembered, after his fall, only by his birth-name Ossun Dray, a Vask-bench journeyman of considerable early promise who was accused by a fellow smith of tampering with a sealed capsule's outer casing under cover of a routine handling error. The evidence was entirely circumstantial - a broken seam on the capsule's outer shell that could have resulted from ordinary mishap, a pattern of behavior in the days before that struck witnesses as evasive, nothing more definitive than that - and Dray maintained his innocence to the end of the proceedings and, by some accounts, for the rest of his life. The Reckoning found against him by a narrow margin, his stylus was broken, and he vanished from guild records thereafter. The case remains a source of quiet unease within the guild to this day, cited by some as proof that the Reckoning process works even under difficult, ambiguous circumstances, and cited by others, in lower voices, as a caution that the guild's hunger for certainty can sometimes convict a man on little more than the shape of his discomfort.

A third and lower-stakes but more frequently recurring dispute concerns pace and availability - essentially, the tension between the guild's insistence on unhurried, properly reverent sealing and the practical impatience of seekers and Warden-Keepers who simply want their capsule closed so a trial can proceed. Every generation of rite-smiths inherits some version of this argument, usually phrased as a disagreement between the guild's older, more traditionalist members, who hold that any pressure to speed a sealing is itself a corruption of the craft, and its younger or more pragmatically minded members, who argue that a guild too precious about its own ceremony risks making itself irrelevant to the very seekers and Keepers it exists to serve. This tension has never produced a schism as dramatic as the bench rivalries or a scandal as dark as the Reckoning of the Sealed Hand, but it simmers constantly beneath the surface of guild politics, flaring up whenever a particularly demanding Keeper or an unusually large influx of seekers puts real strain on a guild-hall's capacity to seal at its accustomed pace.

## VIII. Rite-Smiths and Warden-Keepers

The relationship between the rite-smith guild and the order of Warden-Keepers is, by long tradition, cordial on its surface and quietly wary underneath, the way two old institutions that need each other but do not entirely trust each other's self-regard tend to behave. Neither order can function without the other in any practical sense: the rite-smiths seal the capsules that bind each trial, and the Warden-Keepers are the ones who receive those sealed capsules and stand ready at the threshold of the eight chambers to answer the seekers who come. Yet each order harbors, beneath its public courtesy, a private conviction that its own labor is the truly essential one and the other's, while necessary, is somehow secondary.

The rite-smiths' version of this conviction is rarely stated bluntly but is nonetheless pervasive in guild-hall talk: that the Warden-Keepers, however venerable their own order, are ultimately custodians of something the rite-smiths made, and that a Keeper without a sealed capsule to receive is simply a door-warden with nothing to guard. This sentiment finds its clearest expression in an old guild proverb, repeated with a certain relish among younger rite-smiths though frowned upon as impolitic by senior guild-masters when Keepers might be within earshot: "The Keeper stands at the door; the smith built the room." The proverb is not meant, guild-masters insist when pressed, as any real disrespect to the Keepers' order, whose age and gravity the rite-smiths do genuinely honor, but the fact that it needs to be walked back so often suggests the sentiment behind it runs deeper than the guild likes to admit in mixed company.

The Warden-Keepers, for their part, are said - though naturally this account comes filtered through rite-smith testimony rather than any Keeper's own words, since the Keepers are notoriously reticent about their internal culture - to hold a mirroring conviction that the rite-smiths, for all their beautiful and ancient ceremony, ultimately produce a single sealed object and hand it over, while the Keepers bear the far longer and heavier burden of standing ready at the threshold for however long their tenure demands, answering seeker after seeker, torch after torch, curse after curse, across a span of service the rite-smiths' comparatively episodic labor cannot match. Rite-smiths who have spent time around guild-halls that neighbor a Keeper's threshold report an occasional dry remark from an old Keeper to the effect that "sealing a shell takes an afternoon; keeping a threshold takes a lifetime" - a comment rite-smiths receive, when it reaches them, with visible irritation followed, more often than not, by a grudging private admission that the Keeper has something of a point.

Despite this mutual, mostly good-natured condescension, the two orders have developed over centuries a set of formal courtesies that keep the friction from ever curdling into real hostility. Every guild-hall of any size maintains what is called a Standing Correspondence with the nearest Keeper's threshold, a running exchange of ceremonial greetings, seasonal gifts, and formal notices of apprentices raised or masters retired, conducted with a stiffness that both sides seem to find comforting precisely because it asks so little real warmth of either party. When a new Warden-Keeper takes up a threshold, guild custom calls for the nearest guild-hall to send a delegation bearing a specially prepared ceremonial shell - never a functioning capsule, always an inert, decorative one - as a gesture of welcome, and Keepers are known to display these ceremonial shells prominently near their threshold for the whole of their tenure, whatever private opinion they might hold of the guild that sent it.

There have been, across the long span of guild-Keeper relations, periods of genuine warmth as well as periods of genuine strain, and the guild's oral history preserves stories of both. On the warmer side, there is the well-loved account of a rite-smith named Ilvet Corr, who is remembered for having maintained a decades-long friendship with a Warden-Keeper at a distant threshold, the two of them exchanging letters full of guild gossip and threshold gossip respectively, a friendship so genuine that when Corr died, the Keeper is said to have left the threshold briefly, an almost unheard-of act, to attend the guild funeral and speak in Corr's honor - a story the guild treasures precisely because it is so unusual. On the more strained side, there is the memory of a period called, without much affection, the Long Wrangle, when a string of unusually demanding Keepers at several neighboring thresholds began insisting on sealing timelines the guild considered disrespectfully hurried, provoking a wave of guild-hall grumbling, a few outright refusals by senior smiths to be rushed, and at least one recorded instance of a guild-hall temporarily suspending its Standing Correspondence with a threshold in a fit of collective guild pique, before cooler heads on both sides eventually restored the customary courtesies.

Through all of this friction, warmth, and everything between, one point of genuine, unforced agreement holds the two orders together more firmly than any formal correspondence could: both share an absolute, bone-deep reverence for the irreversibility of a sealed capsule. Whatever else divides them, no rite-smith and no Warden-Keeper has ever been recorded disputing the principle that what is sealed must remain sealed, that no hand from either order has any business attempting to unmake a closing once it is given. This shared conviction is, if the guild's own historians are to be believed, the true and quiet foundation beneath every ceremonial courtesy exchanged between hall and threshold, the one article of faith neither order has ever seriously questioned even in their worst periods of mutual irritation.

## IX. Superstition, Taboo, and the Unspoken Fears

No craft so wrapped in ceremony can avoid accumulating a thick undergrowth of superstition, and the rite-smiths' guild is no exception, harboring a whole ecology of taboos, omens, and half-believed warnings that circulate through guild-halls with a persistence no amount of official guild skepticism has ever managed to root out.

Foremost among these is the near-universal dread of what is called the Second Shell - the superstition, common to smiths of every bench despite their other disagreements, that a rite-smith who is ever asked to reseal a capsule for a trial they have already sealed once before, under any circumstance, however innocent the reason, is courting some obscure and unspecified misfortune. Since capsules once sealed are never reopened and never resealed as a matter of guild principle and, so far as the guild understands, as a matter of the sealing-script's own nature, the Second Shell is in ordinary practice a purely theoretical fear, never actually tested by any real event - which has not stopped generations of apprentices from being warned about it in hushed tones, nor stopped the guild's storytellers from inventing cautionary tales about smiths who supposedly attempted it and came to bad ends, tales that grow more elaborate and less plausible with each retelling and that no serious guild historian believes actually happened, though none will quite say so where a superstitious junior smith might overhear.

A related and more practically observed taboo concerns the naming of an unsealed shell before it is closed. Guild custom strictly forbids referring to any blank capsule-shell awaiting sealing by any name, description, or anticipatory label suggesting what trial it will eventually bind, on the theory - never fully articulated even by its most devout adherents - that naming a thing before it exists in its sealed form somehow presumes upon the sealing itself, tempting a kind of guild-version of bad luck that smiths refer to only obliquely as "waking the shell early." Rite-smiths who slip and refer to a blank shell by anything other than strictly neutral terms are subject to good-natured but insistent correction from their peers, and a persistent offender, particularly an apprentice, may be assigned the mildly punitive chore of sitting a night-vigil with the offending shell as a kind of ritual apology, though nobody in the guild today could explain with a straight face exactly what the vigil is meant to accomplish beyond embarrassing the apprentice into better habits.

More seriously held is the guild's deep unease around any sealing performed by a smith who has recently suffered a significant personal grief or shock, an unease that goes well beyond the Cistern Bench's formal doctrine on the matter and pervades all three benches informally. It is considered, at minimum, poor form, and by more traditional smiths outright dangerous, to ask a recently bereaved or badly shaken rite-smith to seal anything at all until a customary mourning-interval has passed, and guild-halls maintain informal rotations precisely so that no single smith is ever forced, by simple staffing necessity, to seal while in a state the guild broadly terms "unsteadied." The guild has no consistent account of what exactly might go wrong with a seal closed by an unsteadied hand - unlike the Cistern Bench's specific doctrine of a "wound with a lid on it," the wider guild superstition is vaguer, more a generalized dread than a specific claim - but the practice of shielding unsteadied smiths from sealing duty is nearly universal regardless.

Finally, there is the matter of the guild's attitude toward its own dead, which shades from ordinary funerary custom into something closer to superstition at its edges. A rite-smith's stylus, upon their death, is never passed to another smith for continued use, however fine a tool it might be, but is instead either buried or, more commonly among Vask-bench and Doleth-bench traditions, sunk into the deep flooded levels below even the lowest guild-hall, in a rite called the Returning, on the theory that a stylus which has served one hand faithfully for a lifetime has, in some sense the guild does not attempt to fully explain, become an extension of that hand and ought not to be asked to serve another. Cistern-bench tradition differs slightly here, preferring to keep a deceased master's stylus displayed, never used again but never hidden away either, in the guild-hall's small memorial alcove, a difference in practice the other benches note with mild puzzlement but have never made into a serious point of contention, since everyone agrees on the underlying sentiment even where the outward custom diverges.

## X. The Last Sealing and the Rite of Retirement

A rite-smith's career, however long, is understood by the guild to move toward a single foreseeable close, and the manner in which that close is observed has become, over generations, as elaborately ritualized as the Long Silence that opened it. When a smith reaches the age or infirmity at which their hand can no longer be trusted to close a seal with full confidence - a judgment the guild leaves, notably, to the smith's own conscience rather than to any external examiner, on the theory that no one else can feel the absence of the Take as surely as the smith themselves - they are expected to request of their guild-hall what is called the Last Sealing.

The Last Sealing is not treated as a lesser or diminished version of ordinary practice but as its own distinct honor, attended by as much ceremony as the First True Sealing that began the smith's working life, though the mood of the two occasions could not be more different. Where the First True Sealing is watched in tense, hopeful silence, the Last Sealing is attended almost festively, with the whole guild-hall's membership crowding in to watch, apprentices given special dispensation to observe despite the ordinary strictures of the Long Silence, and a shared meal prepared beforehand so that the sealing itself becomes only the solemn centerpiece of what is otherwise closer to a celebration than a rite. The shell chosen for a Last Sealing is, by long custom, one the retiring smith selects personally, often weeks or months in advance, and guild gossip delights in speculating which shell a beloved master will choose, since the choice is considered a kind of final statement of character, though what exactly a shell can be said to express is a question the guild has never resolved and probably never will.

After the Last Sealing is closed and judged, by the same silent inspection that governs every other sealing, the retiring smith's guild-name is not retired alongside them, as one might expect, but is instead formally passed into what the guild calls the Long Roll, a spoken and, in the larger halls, physically inscribed record of every rite-smith who has ever completed a Last Sealing in good standing. To have one's name entered on the Long Roll is considered the single highest honor the guild can bestow, exceeding even the fame of a Corvain Ashwrist or a Yeselin Doura, precisely because it cannot be earned by any single spectacular feat but only by a full working lifetime carried through without disgrace. Smiths broken by the Reckoning of the Sealed Hand are, naturally, barred from the Long Roll forever, their names struck if they had already been entered, which is one further reason the Reckoning is regarded with such dread throughout the guild.

Following the Last Sealing, a retired smith does not vanish from guild life but takes up what is informally called the Watching Bench - not a fourth tradition of craft alongside Vask, Doleth, and Cistern, but simply the customary place, physically a raised stone seat at the rear of most guild-halls, where retired smiths are expected to sit during apprentices' training sessions and offer commentary the acting masters cannot, since a retired smith no longer has any stake in bench rivalry or in the reputation of their own current pupils and can therefore, in theory, judge a young apprentice's work with a disinterest no active master fully possesses. Some of the guild's most quoted maxims about the craft are attributed not to famous sealers at all but to anonymous voices from the Watching Bench, a fact that has led to a small, affectionate guild joke that the truest wisdom about sealing is only ever spoken by those who no longer do it.

## XI. Regional Variation and the Far Halls

Everything recounted so far in this book describes the guild culture as it is generally practiced and generally understood across the greater number of guild-halls clustered nearest the older, more central drowned districts, where the Three-Thirds House and the memory of the founding three hold the strongest sway. It would be a disservice to the guild's real diversity, however, to leave the impression that rite-smith culture is perfectly uniform across every hall that claims the name. The far halls - guild-halls established generations after the founding, in more remote or more recently resettled drowned districts, sometimes at a distance from the Three-Thirds House that a messenger might take a full season to cross - have developed local customs that the central halls regard with a mixture of fond indulgence and mild scandal, depending on how far the local practice has strayed from what the central tradition considers proper form.

Chief among these far-hall variations is the practice, unknown in any central guild-hall, of the so-called Twin Witness, observed in a cluster of far halls said to have been founded by a group of Cistern-bench emigrants some generations back. In these halls, no sealing of any consequence is performed by a single smith at all, however senior; every sealing is instead carried out by two smiths working in close physical proximity, one performing the actual closing while the other stands as a permanent, unbroken witness whose sole task is to attest afterward, in a formal statement recorded by the guild-hall's keeper of records, that they observed nothing amiss in the sealing smith's bearing or manner throughout. Central-hall traditionalists consider this practice faintly insulting to the individual smith's competence, muttering that a properly trained rite-smith ought not need a permanent chaperone to close a simple seal, but the far halls that practice it defend it warmly, arguing that it merely extends the logic of the guild's own Reckoning process into ordinary daily use, catching any trouble before it can ever become the subject of a formal accusation at all.

Other far halls have developed variations less structural and more atmospheric: a hall in one of the deeper eastern flood-districts is said to perform every sealing by the light of a single specific kind of slow-burning taper found nowhere else, claiming, without any central-hall smith ever having been able to confirm or deny the claim firsthand, that the particular quality of that light aids the Take in some fashion no other illumination can match. Another far hall, smaller and poorer than most, is remembered fondly across the wider guild for having abolished the formal Naming of Hands recitation entirely in favor of a much shorter private nod each smith makes toward a single modest plaque bearing all the founders' names at once, a practical adaptation the hall's founders are said to have adopted simply because their guild-hall was too small and too frequently in use to spare the time for the fuller recitation before every sealing, and which the central halls, when they eventually learned of it, chose to treat as an endearing regional quirk rather than a violation worth disciplining.

These regional differences, however they might read to an outsider as evidence of a guild fraying at its edges, are understood within the guild itself as proof of something closer to the opposite: that the sealing-script and the reverence surrounding it have proven resilient enough to take root and flourish under an enormous range of local conditions without ever losing their essential shape. A rite-smith from the Three-Thirds House and a rite-smith from the farthest eastern flood-district, meeting for the first time and possessing between them scarcely a custom or a turn of phrase in common, will nonetheless recognize each other instantly and completely the moment either one speaks the fixed formula of the Passing of the Shell, and it is this recognition, more than any central authority or written charter, that the guild considers the true and sufficient proof of its own unbroken continuity across all the scattered, drowned centuries of its long life.

## XII. The Guild's Present Standing

At the present hour of the drowned world's long twilight, the rite-smith guild persists much as it has for longer than any single living smith can trace with certainty, its halls scattered through the flooded levels in loose affiliation with one another, its three benches still arguing the same argument the Debate of the Three Hands never quite settled, its apprentices still enduring the Long Silence, its masters still closing seals with the same fixed formula that has passed from mouth to mouth since the Cistern of First Accord. Seekers who come to the maze rarely think much, if at all, about the guild whose labor made their trial possible in the first place, and the rite-smiths, for their part, would likely tell you they prefer it that way. Theirs is not a craft that seeks the seeker's gratitude. It seeks only, in the words repeated at the close of every First True Sealing across every bench and every guild-hall without exception, to carry the mark that was given, faithfully, quietly, for as long as there remains a mark to carry and a hand willing to learn it.

It is worth noting, in closing this account, that the guild itself would likely object to nearly everything this book has just said about it, not because any of it is false so much as because the guild holds, as an article of faith nearly as old as the founding oath itself, that its true nature cannot be adequately conveyed to anyone who has not sat through the Long Silence themselves. Every rite-smith who has ever spoken to an outside chronicler, and there have been remarkably few willing to do so at any length, has ended the conversation with some version of the same caveat: that words can gesture toward the craft but cannot replace the years of watching, waiting, and finally feeling the Take beneath one's own hand. This book, then, should be read as such a gesture, offered in the same spirit of humble stewardship the rite-smiths themselves claim toward the sealing-script they carry - an attempt to carry forward, as faithfully as an outsider's hand can manage, a little of what was given.
