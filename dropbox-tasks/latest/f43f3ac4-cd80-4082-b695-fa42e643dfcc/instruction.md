A dendrochronology lab is planning this season's increment coring across forty tree-ring sites, `ts-01` through `ts-40`, listed under /app/environment/data. Each site holds a floating ring sequence that is worthless until it is pinned to the regional master, and pinning it takes a single core whose series actually crossdates. Bore a tree and its core either carries a clean, sensitive run of rings that lands cleanly against the master, or it comes up rotten, ring-broken, or too flat to match and settles nothing. That a given core crossdates is chance, governed by a per-core rate `p` fixed by the site, and cores are independent, so committing `n` of them pins a site with probability `1 - (1 - p)^n`; a lone crossdatable series does it. For every site you choose two numbers, cores booked on the cheap shared field crew and cores booked on a dearer contract corer, and you want the total spend as low as it will go while each site still reaches the `anchor_floor` set in /app/environment/data/chronology_policy.json. A starter `dater` already sits in /app/environment, sizing each site from its own past cores alone, and the point is to do better than that. The catch lives in /app/environment/data/core_log.csv. A thin record pins almost nothing: a site whose two logged cores both crossdated can read far stronger than a season will bear out, while the flattest, most complacent sites were bored over and over with never a series landing, so their tally is nearly mute on the rate a fresh season will draw. The fresh season is what the score uses. Each site's real per-core rate for it is held back, and the plan's `1 - (1 - p)^n` gets rebuilt on those unseen rates rather than on anything the log shows. Scoring runs site by site and hangs on whichever site comes out weakest, so margin stacked on the sites you already trust buys nothing once a single site falls short. A plan that survives leans on the trend running across the whole network instead of each bare tally, and it spends its widest margin exactly where a site's record leaves the rate most unsettled.

Cores reach the ground two ways. The field crew is cheap at `field_cost` each, but every field core it cuts is drawn from one shared pool, `field_core_budget` in that same policy file, that all forty sites split between them, so once the pool empties a site's remaining cores pass to the contract corer at the higher `contract_cost`. A plan's committed cost is `field cores x field_cost + contract cores x contract_cost` totalled over the sites, held under the `cost_ceiling` given in that same policy file. Genuine slack sits under that ceiling, and it is meant for depth: pour extra cores into the few sites whose rate you cannot yet pin down and the budget takes it in stride. What it will not take is that same depth applied to all forty together, where the little overages sum past the cap. Overrun the ceiling and the plan is refused; let one site finish under the floor and it is refused the same way. The one thing the files withhold is the set of true per-core rates.

Write the dater in Rust in /app/environment. A `make` build yields `bin/dater`; called as `./bin/dater data` it consumes /app/environment/data and prints to stdout a single JSON object, a `plan` array whose entries read `{"site_id": ..., "field_cores": ..., "contract_cores": ...}`, one for each site, every count a non-negative whole number. Because grading recompiles the source and runs the binary afresh, the counts must be computed by the program, never pasted in as literals. The toolchain is already in place and nothing reaches the network; the input columns are laid out in /app/environment/data/README.md.
