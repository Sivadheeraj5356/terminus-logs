A power-semiconductor line does not put an IGBT die design into production on a hunch. A die earns its design a place only by passing the whole acceptance in a single sitting: it holds off the rated collector-emitter voltage in the blocking state, keeps its on-state drop inside the saturation window, and rides out a short-circuit pulse without latching. Fail any one of the three and it is scrapped. How readily a design yields a die that clears all three is fixed by how the design landed in fab, a per-die rate `p`; build `n` dies and the design is released the moment one of them passes, at `1 - (1 - p)^n`.

Forty candidate designs are on this slate, `ig-01` through `ig-40` under /app/environment/data. For each one you set two counts: how many dies to carry on the fab's own in-house probe, and how many to hand to an outside test house. Both routes run the identical acceptance, so a die's chance of passing is the same either way; the routes differ only in price and in how many the cheap one will take. The starter `switcher` in /app/environment takes the easy road, pinning each design's rate to that design's own thin log and nothing else.

The score comes from a future production run, not from the log on disk. For that run every design has a true acceptance rate you never get to see, and the grade puts your count and that rate back through `1 - (1 - p)^n`. Miss the floor on a single design and the whole slate falls: all forty have to clear the `signoff_floor` in /app/environment/data/release_rule.json together on the unseen run, and it is the poorest design that the plan is marked on. That throws the weight onto the record, and the record is weakest where it counts. The designs most likely to disappoint are the ones /app/environment/data/test_log.csv has only a die or two on, sometimes none, and so few results settle almost nothing about a rate. Trust a count to two or three such results and the design behind it gives way the moment real dies come off the line. A short record that read well settles almost nothing. Where the acceptance truly lands can sit a great deal lower than the log lets on, so the plan carries margin for that gap, and carries it widest on the designs the record barely covers.

Spend adds up `probe_cost` for every in-house die and `lab_cost` for every outside one across the forty designs, and it has to land under a fixed ceiling you are never shown. The ceiling is not miserly. There is room in it, on purpose, to build a genuinely doubtful design as deep as the floor needs, and doing that is the intended move rather than an extravagance. Where it gives out is on cushion spread evenly: add a few dies to every design at once, the ones you trust no less than the ones you do not, and the bill clears the ceiling. So the ceiling and the floor pull against each other, and a plan has to answer both. The in-house probe has a cap of its own, a `probe_capacity` of dies split across all forty, and once that is used up the rest of a design's dies fall to the pricier outside house. Neither the true rates nor the ceiling is a number handed to you.

Work the plan out in Rust under /app/environment. `make` builds `bin/switcher`, and `./bin/switcher data` reads the files under /app/environment/data and prints the plan to stdout as JSON: an object with a `plan` array of entries `{"design_id": ..., "probe_dies": ..., "lab_dies": ...}`, one per design, every count a non-negative integer. Grading rebuilds `bin/switcher` from your source with `make` and runs it, so the plan has to fall out of the Rust and not be typed in by hand. The Rust toolchain is installed and on the path; there is no network. The files are described in /app/environment/data/README.md.
